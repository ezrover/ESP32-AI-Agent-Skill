# Lower Back — Exercise Knowledge Base
Extraction date: 2026-08-03. Sourcing rules: see knowledge-base/README.md.
Corroborated exercises: 26. Candidates: 4.

Pain group: non-specific low back pain, core stability, lumbar mobility, posture.
General guidance repeated across Tier 1 sources: keep moving rather than resting, start gently, expect mild muscle soreness (not sharp pain), warm up with 5–10 minutes of light activity, and stop any movement that sends pain into a leg or clearly worsens symptoms.

## Stretches & Mobility

### Knee-to-Chest Stretch
- **Aliases:** single knee-to-chest, double knee-to-chest, knee hug
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** lumbar extensors (erector spinae), gluteals, hip
- **Purpose:** Gently opens up the lower back and eases tension in the lumbar and gluteal muscles. A first-line, low-load stretch for stiff or achy backs and a core movement of the Williams flexion series.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back with both knees bent and feet flat on the floor.
  2. Bring one knee up toward your chest.
  3. Wrap both hands around the back of the thigh or over the shin and draw the knee gently closer.
  4. Let your lower back relax and stay heavy against the floor.
  5. Hold, then lower the leg back to the start with control.
  6. Repeat on the other side; as a progression, hug both knees at once.
- **Form cues:** Keep the resting foot flat or leg long on the floor; pull with the arms, not by yanking the neck forward; keep shoulders and head relaxed on the floor.
- **Common mistakes:** Lifting the head and straining the neck; pulling so hard the pelvis curls sharply off the floor; holding the breath.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 10–30 seconds, 2–5 reps per side, 1–2 sessions daily.
- **Progression:** Both knees to chest together; add a gentle rock. | **Regression:** Smaller range; or do a seated knee-to-chest from a chair.
- **Cautions:** Skip or shorten the range if it causes hip pinching or sends symptoms down a leg; recent hip replacement users should follow their own movement guidance.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — supine floor exercise; from a propped-up front camera the motion is foreshortened and mostly hidden (a side-on floor placement could see hip-flexion angle, but not the default view).
- **Sources:** [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/adult-health/in-depth/back-pain/art-20546859) (T1); [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/recovery/spine-conditioning-program/) (T1); [StatPearls — Williams Back Exercises](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551558/) (T1); [Versus Arthritis](https://versusarthritis.org/about-arthritis/healthcare-professionals/training-and-education-resources/useful-resources/back-pain-exercises/) (T1); [AskDoctorJo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/back-pain-relief-seated/) (T3)

### Lower Trunk Rotation
- **Aliases:** knee rolls, lower back rotational stretch, lumbar rotation, windshield wipers
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** lumbar rotators, obliques, paraspinals
- **Purpose:** Restores gentle rotational mobility in the lumbar spine and eases stiffness. Frequently prescribed by NHS physiotherapy services as an early-morning "loosener."
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back with knees bent, feet flat, and arms out to the sides for balance.
  2. Keep knees and feet together.
  3. Slowly lower both knees toward one side as far as comfortable.
  4. Pause a few seconds, feeling a gentle stretch across the lower back.
  5. Use your abdominals to bring the knees back to the middle.
  6. Repeat to the other side, alternating.
- **Form cues:** Keep both shoulders in contact with the floor; move slowly and let gravity do the work; only go as far as comfortable rotation allows.
- **Common mistakes:** Letting a shoulder lift and turning the whole trunk; dropping the knees quickly instead of controlling them; forcing range through pain.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 5–10 seconds per side, 5–10 reps each side (NHS leaflets suggest up to 30 gentle alternations), 1–2 times daily.
- **Progression:** Longer holds at end range; straighten legs slightly for more lever. | **Regression:** Smaller arc of motion; a pillow under the knees on each side.
- **Cautions:** Ease off if rotation reproduces leg symptoms or sharp one-sided pain.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — supine floor movement; the knee sway happens low and parallel to the floor and is unreliable from a propped front camera.
- **Sources:** [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/adult-health/in-depth/back-pain/art-20546859) (T1); [NHS Ayrshire & Arran](https://www.nhsaaa.net/musculoskeletal-msk-service-patient-portal/low-back-pain-msk-patient-portal/low-back-pain-exercises-msk-patient-portal/) (T1); [NHS Inform](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/muscle-bone-and-joints/neck-and-back-problems-and-conditions/exercises-for-back-pain/) (T1); [Versus Arthritis](https://versusarthritis.org/about-arthritis/healthcare-professionals/training-and-education-resources/useful-resources/back-pain-exercises/) (T1)

### Cat-Cow
- **Aliases:** cat stretch, cat-camel, arching and hollowing, Marjaryasana-Bitilasana
- **Position:** quadruped
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** whole spine (lumbar and thoracic mobility), deep abdominals, paraspinals
- **Purpose:** Cycles the spine gently between flexion and extension to reduce stiffness and improve segmental mobility. Also used by McGill-style programs as a low-load warm-up before core work.
- **Steps:**
  1. Start on hands and knees, hands under shoulders, knees under hips.
  2. Exhale and round the back up toward the ceiling, tucking the chin and tailbone (cat).
  3. Hold for a breath.
  4. Inhale and let the belly sink, lifting the chest and tailbone so the spine forms a gentle bow (cow).
  5. Hold for a breath.
  6. Flow slowly between the two shapes.
- **Form cues:** Move with the breath; distribute the curve along the whole spine rather than hinging at one spot; keep elbows soft and shoulders away from the ears.
- **Common mistakes:** Forcing end-range extension and pinching the low back; moving fast and skipping the mid-back; collapsing into the shoulders.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 slow cycles, 1–3 sets, once or twice daily.
- **Progression:** Longer holds at each end; add gentle breath-paced tempo work. | **Regression:** Smaller arcs ("comfortable mid-range only"); forearms on a bench if wrists hurt.
- **Cautions:** Keep the range small if either extreme provokes pain; use padding under sore knees.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — quadruped floor pose seen end-on from a propped front camera; spinal curvature change is a side-view signal.
- **Sources:** [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/adult-health/in-depth/back-pain/art-20546859) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/cat-cow-stretch) (T1); [Versus Arthritis](https://versusarthritis.org/about-arthritis/healthcare-professionals/training-and-education-resources/useful-resources/back-pain-exercises/) (T1); [SquatUniversity (McGill warm-up)](https://squatuniversity.com/2018/06/21/the-mcgill-big-3-for-core-stability/) (T3)

### Child's Pose
- **Aliases:** bottom-to-heels stretch, prayer stretch, Balasana
- **Position:** quadruped
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** lumbar extensors, lats, gluteals, hips
- **Purpose:** A restful sustained flexion stretch that decompresses and lengthens the lower back musculature. Commonly used to calm a stiff, achy back and as a break between strengthening sets.
- **Steps:**
  1. Kneel on all fours with knees about hip-width (or slightly wider) apart.
  2. Reach both arms out along the floor in front of you.
  3. Slowly sink your hips back toward your heels.
  4. Rest your forehead toward the floor and let the back lengthen.
  5. Breathe slowly, letting the trunk relax a little more with each exhale.
  6. Walk the hands back in to return to all fours.
- **Form cues:** Keep arms long and reach through the fingertips; let the hips do the moving; breathe into the back of the ribs.
- **Common mistakes:** Holding tension in the shoulders and neck; bouncing instead of a relaxed hold; forcing the hips fully to the heels despite knee or hip discomfort.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 10–30 seconds (or ~5 slow breaths), 3–5 reps, 1–2 times daily.
- **Progression:** Walk hands to each side for an added lateral stretch. | **Regression:** Pillow between hips and heels or under the chest; smaller sit-back range.
- **Cautions:** Modify or skip with significant knee problems; ease off if deep flexion increases symptoms.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — low, folded floor posture; joint landmarks are occluded from a front camera.
- **Sources:** [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/exercises-and-stretches-for-low-back-pain) (T1); [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/pain/stretching-and-strengthening-exercises-to-relieve-and-prevent-lower-back-pain) (T1); [NHS Ayrshire & Arran (bottom to heels)](https://www.nhsaaa.net/musculoskeletal-msk-service-patient-portal/low-back-pain-msk-patient-portal/low-back-pain-exercises-msk-patient-portal/) (T1)

### Supine Hamstring Stretch
- **Aliases:** lying hamstring stretch, towel hamstring stretch, straight-leg raise stretch
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** none | towel (optional strap)
- **Target:** hamstrings; secondary relief for lumbar extensors
- **Purpose:** Tight hamstrings tug the pelvis into positions that load the lower back; lengthening them reduces strain during bending and sitting. Included in the AAOS spine program and the Williams flexion series.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back with both knees bent, feet flat.
  2. Lift one leg and hold behind the thigh with both hands (or loop a towel over the sole of the foot).
  3. Starting with the knee bent, slowly straighten the knee toward the ceiling.
  4. Stop when you feel a comfortable stretch in the back of the thigh.
  5. Hold, breathing normally, then bend the knee and lower the leg.
  6. Repeat on the other side.
- **Form cues:** Keep the low back and hips settled on the floor; a soft knee is fine — chase the thigh-back stretch, not a locked knee; keep the opposite foot flat.
- **Common mistakes:** Bouncing; lifting the hips off the floor to force the leg vertical; pulling the neck up to watch the foot.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 15–30 seconds, 2–4 reps per leg, daily.
- **Progression:** Straighter knee, leg closer to vertical; gentle ankle pumps at end range. | **Regression:** Towel/strap assist; do it in a doorway resting the leg on the frame; seated version from a chair.
- **Cautions:** Stop if it produces nerve-type symptoms (tingling/shooting pain) down the leg rather than a muscle stretch.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — supine floor stretch; leg elevation toward the camera is heavily foreshortened from a propped front view.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/recovery/spine-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Mayo Clinic Health System PDF](https://www.mayoclinichealthsystem.org/-/media/national-files/documents/hometown-health/2021/low-back-pain-exercises.pdf) (T1); [StatPearls — Williams Back Exercises](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551558/) (T1); [AskDoctorJo (seated variant)](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/back-pain-relief-seated/) (T3)

### Kneeling Hip Flexor Stretch
- **Aliases:** half-kneeling psoas stretch, lunge stretch, iliopsoas stretch
- **Position:** standing (half-kneeling)
- **Equipment:** none | towel (knee padding)
- **Target:** hip flexors (iliopsoas, rectus femoris)
- **Purpose:** Tight hip flexors tilt the pelvis forward and increase lumbar arch, a common contributor to low back ache in people who sit a lot. Lengthening them reduces that pull; part of the Williams series and NASM's corrective approach.
- **Steps:**
  1. Kneel on one knee with the other foot planted in front, both knees near 90 degrees.
  2. Tuck the tailbone slightly under by squeezing the glute of the kneeling side.
  3. Keeping the trunk tall, shift your weight gently forward onto the front foot.
  4. Feel the stretch across the front of the kneeling hip.
  5. Hold, then ease back and switch sides.
- **Form cues:** Squeeze the rear glute to focus the stretch; ribs stacked over pelvis — stay tall; shift forward from the hips, not by arching the back.
- **Common mistakes:** Letting the low back over-arch (which fakes the stretch); leaning the chest forward; front knee collapsing past the toes uncontrolled.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 30–45 seconds, 2–4 reps per side, most days.
- **Progression:** Raise the same-side arm overhead and add a slight lean away. | **Regression:** Standing version with rear foot on the floor; hold a chair for balance.
- **Cautions:** Pad the knee; skip the deep position if the kneecap is sensitive or balance is poor.
- **Camera trackability:** trackable-hold — the half-kneeling stance is upright and visible from a front camera; a pose model can verify the split-knee configuration and upright trunk, though the subtle pelvic tilt itself is not measurable.
- **Sources:** [StatPearls — Williams Back Exercises](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551558/) (T1); [Mayo Clinic Health System PDF](https://www.mayoclinichealthsystem.org/-/media/national-files/documents/hometown-health/2021/low-back-pain-exercises.pdf) (T1); [NASM blog](https://blog.nasm.org/exercises-for-lower-back-pain) (T2); [ACE exercise library](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/142/kneeling-hip-flexor-stretch/) (T2)

### Figure-4 Piriformis Stretch
- **Aliases:** supine piriformis stretch, figure-four, crossed-leg glute stretch
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** piriformis, deep hip rotators, gluteals
- **Purpose:** Releases tension in the deep buttock muscles that often accompany (and refer pain into) the lower back and can irritate the sciatic nerve. AAOS includes a rotation-stretch equivalent in its spine program.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back with knees bent, feet flat.
  2. Cross one ankle over the opposite knee, letting the crossed knee fall outward (a "4" shape).
  3. Reach through the gap and clasp behind the thigh of the supporting leg.
  4. Draw both legs gently toward your chest.
  5. Hold when you feel a stretch deep in the buttock of the crossed leg.
  6. Release slowly and swap sides.
- **Form cues:** Keep the head and shoulders on the floor; gently press the crossed knee away to deepen the stretch; keep breathing slow and steady.
- **Common mistakes:** Rounding the neck up toward the legs; pulling hard enough to pinch the front of the hip; letting the pelvis twist off the floor.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 20–30 seconds, 2–3 reps per side, daily.
- **Progression:** Pull the legs closer; seated upright figure-4 with forward hinge. | **Regression:** Rest the supporting foot on a wall instead of holding the thigh; seated chair version.
- **Cautions:** Stop if it causes tingling or numbness down the leg; keep the range small after recent hip surgery.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — supine floor stretch with crossed legs; landmark occlusion and foreshortening make it unreliable from a front camera.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/recovery/spine-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/piriformis-syndrome-stretches-exercises) (T1); [Hinge Health](https://www.hingehealth.com/resources/articles/figure-four/) (T2); [AskDoctorJo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/lower-back-pain-relief/) (T3)

### Seated Lower-Back Rotation Stretch
- **Aliases:** seated spinal twist, sitting rotation stretch, chair twist
- **Position:** seated
- **Equipment:** chair
- **Target:** lumbar and thoracic rotators, obliques, piriformis
- **Purpose:** Maintains rotational mobility of the spine from a supported position — useful at a desk or for people who find floor work difficult. Appears in Mayo's 15-minute back routine and the AAOS program.
- **Steps:**
  1. Sit tall on an armless chair or stool, feet flat.
  2. Cross one leg over the other (or keep feet planted for a gentler version).
  3. Brace the opposite elbow or hand against the outside of the raised knee.
  4. Slowly turn your trunk toward the crossed-leg side, leading with the breastbone.
  5. Hold, keeping hips facing forward.
  6. Return to center and repeat the other way.
- **Form cues:** Sit tall before you twist — rotate around a long spine; turn from the mid-back, letting the head follow last; keep both sit bones planted.
- **Common mistakes:** Slumping then twisting; wrenching further with the arms; lifting a hip off the seat.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold ~10 seconds, 3–5 reps per side, up to twice daily.
- **Progression:** Deeper rotation with a longer hold. | **Regression:** Feet flat (no leg cross), smaller turn.
- **Cautions:** Keep rotation gentle if it provokes sharp pain; avoid combining a deep twist with a slump.
- **Camera trackability:** trackable-reps — seated upright and facing the phone; shoulder-line rotation (apparent shoulder-width compression and left/right shoulder depth change) is a usable per-rep signal, though rotation depth is only approximate from a single front view.
- **Sources:** [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/adult-health/in-depth/back-pain/art-20546859) (T1); [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/recovery/spine-conditioning-program/) (T1); [AskDoctorJo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/back-pain-relief-seated/) (T3)

### Standing Side Bend
- **Aliases:** lateral trunk flexion, side glide/side flexion mobility, hand-down-thigh stretch
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** none | wall (optional support)
- **Target:** quadratus lumborum, obliques, lateral trunk
- **Purpose:** Keeps side-bending mobility in the lumbar spine and stretches the flank muscles that stiffen with prolonged sitting or guarding. A staple of NHS general back-mobility leaflets.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand tall, feet hip-width apart, arms at your sides.
  2. Slide one hand down the outside of the same-side thigh toward the knee.
  3. Let the trunk bend directly sideways — no leaning forward or back.
  4. Pause briefly at a comfortable end point, feeling a stretch on the opposite flank.
  5. Return to upright using the waist muscles.
  6. Alternate sides in a slow rhythm.
- **Form cues:** Imagine sliding between two panes of glass (pure sideways motion); keep both feet evenly weighted; let the neck follow the spine naturally.
- **Common mistakes:** Twisting or leaning forward as you bend; shifting the hips sideways instead of bending the trunk; rushing the reps.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 reps per side, 1–2 sets, daily; holds of 5–10 seconds optional.
- **Progression:** Reach the opposite arm overhead for a longer lever. | **Regression:** Smaller range; fingertips on a wall for balance.
- **Cautions:** Ease off if one direction consistently reproduces sharp or radiating pain.
- **Camera trackability:** trackable-reps — excellent front-camera exercise: lateral trunk lean is in the camera's plane; shoulder-hip midline angle (frontal-plane trunk tilt) gives a clean per-rep signal.
- **Sources:** [East Lancashire Hospitals NHS](https://elht.nhs.uk/services/integrated-msk-pain-and-rheumatology-service/patient-information/spinal-pain/back-pain-general-mobility-exercises) (T1); [Cambridge University Hospitals NHS](https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/back-mobility-exercises/) (T1); [Bob & Brad](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NM3z8oQsK_U) (T3)

## Strengthening & Stability

### Pelvic Tilt
- **Aliases:** posterior pelvic tilt, lower back flexibility exercise, Williams flexion exercise #1
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** deep abdominals (transverse abdominis), lumbar mobility, gluteals
- **Purpose:** Teaches control of the pelvis and gently mobilizes the lumbar segments while waking up the deep abdominals — the usual first step of nearly every low-back program (NHS, Cleveland Clinic, Williams).
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back with knees bent, feet flat, arms relaxed.
  2. Tighten your stomach muscles and gently flatten your lower back into the floor, tilting the pelvis.
  3. Feel the tailbone curl slightly upward.
  4. Hold while breathing normally.
  5. Relax back to the neutral starting arch.
  6. Repeat in a slow rhythm.
- **Form cues:** Think "belt buckle toward ribs"; the movement is small — pelvis only, hips stay down; keep breathing throughout the hold.
- **Common mistakes:** Pushing through the legs and lifting the hips (turning it into a bridge); holding the breath; clenching shoulders and neck.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 5–10 seconds, 8–12 reps, 1–3 sets, daily.
- **Progression:** Longer holds; perform against a wall while standing; progress to dead bug. | **Regression:** Shorter holds; smaller tilt.
- **Cautions:** Very low-risk; keep it comfortable and small.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — supine with a movement of only a few centimeters at the pelvis; too subtle for front-camera pose landmarks.
- **Sources:** [NHS Inform](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/muscle-bone-and-joints/neck-and-back-problems-and-conditions/exercises-for-back-pain/) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/exercises-and-stretches-for-low-back-pain) (T1); [StatPearls — Williams Back Exercises](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551558/) (T1); [Versus Arthritis](https://versusarthritis.org/about-arthritis/exercising-with-arthritis/exercises-for-healthy-joints/exercises-for-the-back/) (T1); [AskDoctorJo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/lower-back-pain-relief/) (T3)

### Glute Bridge
- **Aliases:** bridge, hip bridge, pelvic lift, hip raise
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** gluteus maximus, hamstrings, lumbar extensors, deep core
- **Purpose:** Strengthens the glutes so they share load the lower back would otherwise absorb, and trains hip extension without spinal strain. Recommended by virtually every Tier 1 program for this pain group.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat and hip-width apart, arms by your sides.
  2. Gently brace the abdominals and squeeze the buttocks.
  3. Push through the heels and lift the hips until shoulders, hips, and knees form a straight line.
  4. Hold at the top, keeping ribs down.
  5. Lower slowly with control.
  6. Repeat.
- **Form cues:** Lead with a glute squeeze, not a back arch; straight line shoulder–hip–knee at the top; press evenly through both heels.
- **Common mistakes:** Over-arching the low back at the top; pushing through the toes; letting the knees splay out or cave in.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 5–10 seconds at top, 8–12 reps, 1–3 sets, daily to most days (Mayo suggests building from 5 toward 30 reps over time).
- **Progression:** Marching bridge, single-leg bridge, feet on a step. | **Regression:** Smaller lift height; shorter holds.
- **Cautions:** Keep the lift height glute-driven; stop if cramping or back pinching occurs at the top.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — supine floor exercise; hip elevation is a side-view signal and is largely invisible from a propped-up front camera facing the user's feet or head.
- **Sources:** [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/adult-health/in-depth/back-pain/art-20546859) (T1); [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/recovery/spine-conditioning-program/) (T1); [NHS Ayrshire & Arran](https://www.nhsaaa.net/musculoskeletal-msk-service-patient-portal/low-back-pain-msk-patient-portal/low-back-pain-exercises-msk-patient-portal/) (T1); [NASM blog](https://blog.nasm.org/exercises-for-lower-back-pain) (T2); [AskDoctorJo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/lower-back-pain-relief/) (T3)

### Bird Dog
- **Aliases:** alternate arm and leg raise, quadruped opposite arm/leg reach, pointer dog
- **Position:** quadruped
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** erector spinae, multifidus, gluteals, abdominals (anti-rotation core)
- **Purpose:** Trains the back and core to hold the spine steady while the limbs move — the key skill for pain-free lifting and walking. One of McGill's "Big Three" and included in AAOS, Kaiser, and ACSM progressions.
- **Steps:**
  1. Start on hands and knees, hands under shoulders, knees under hips, spine neutral.
  2. Draw the belly button gently toward the spine and keep breathing.
  3. Reach one arm straight ahead while extending the opposite leg straight back.
  4. Hold, keeping hips and shoulders level and back flat.
  5. Return the limbs slowly without letting the trunk sway.
  6. Repeat with the opposite pair.
- **Form cues:** Imagine balancing a cup of water on your low back; reach long ("make yourself longer, not higher"); keep the raised leg at hip height, no higher.
- **Common mistakes:** Rotating or hiking the pelvis; arching the low back as the leg lifts; lifting the head to look forward.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 second holds, 5–10 reps per side (McGill-style descending pyramid such as 5-3-1), 1–3 sets, most days.
- **Progression:** Longer holds; draw small squares with the extended hand/foot; hand-to-knee touch under the trunk between reps. | **Regression:** Arm-only or leg-only lifts; toes stay in floor contact.
- **Cautions:** Keep the range where the back stays flat; pad sore knees or wrists.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — quadruped, usually seen end-on or obliquely from a propped phone; opposite-limb extension is best judged from the side, so front-camera rep detection is unreliable.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/recovery/spine-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/staying-healthy/three-moves-for-better-spine-health) (T1); [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.low-back-pain-exercises.ad1482) (T1); [ACSM Health & Fitness Journal](https://journals.lww.com/acsm-healthfitness/fulltext/2013/03000/low_back_pain.4.aspx) (T2); [NASM blog](https://blog.nasm.org/exercises-for-lower-back-pain) (T2); [SquatUniversity](https://squatuniversity.com/2018/06/21/the-mcgill-big-3-for-core-stability/) (T3)

### McGill Curl-Up
- **Aliases:** modified curl-up, McGill crunch (Big Three #1)
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** rectus abdominis and deep core, with the lumbar spine kept neutral
- **Purpose:** Builds anterior core endurance without repeatedly bending the lumbar spine — designed specifically for backs that dislike flexion. Part of the McGill Big Three endorsed by Harvard-affiliated physical therapists.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back with one knee bent, foot flat, and the other leg straight.
  2. Slide both hands (palms down) under the natural arch of your lower back.
  3. Keep the elbows resting on the floor.
  4. Lift only the head and shoulders a few centimeters, as one stiff unit, without flattening the back onto your hands.
  5. Hold ~8–10 seconds, breathing steadily.
  6. Lower slowly; switch the bent leg halfway through the set.
- **Form cues:** The neck stays long — imagine your head resting on a scale and barely unweighting it; no chin-poking; the low back keeps its small arch throughout.
- **Common mistakes:** Turning it into a full crunch with lumbar flexion; jutting the chin; holding the breath.
- **Typical dosage:** 8–10 second holds in a descending pyramid (e.g. 5, then 3, then 1 rep), daily.
- **Progression:** Lift the elbows off the floor; longer total time under tension. | **Regression:** Shorter holds; head lift only.
- **Cautions:** Keep the lift small; stop if neck strain dominates.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — supine with a deliberately tiny head/shoulder lift; motion amplitude is below what a propped front camera can reliably score.
- **Sources:** [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/staying-healthy/three-moves-for-better-spine-health) (T1); [BackFitPro (McGill)](https://www.backfitpro.com/mastering-the-mcgill-big-three-progressions-variations-and-common-pitfalls/) (T3); [SquatUniversity](https://squatuniversity.com/2018/06/21/the-mcgill-big-3-for-core-stability/) (T3)

### Partial Curl-Up
- **Aliases:** partial sit-up, abdominal curl, crunch (partial), Williams partial sit-up
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** rectus abdominis, obliques
- **Purpose:** Strengthens the abdominals that share spinal load, using a short range considered back-friendly by clinical sources (Kaiser, HealthLink BC, Williams protocol).
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back with knees bent, feet flat.
  2. Cross arms over the chest (or lightly support the neck with fingertips, elbows wide).
  3. Tighten the stomach and exhale as you curl the head and shoulder blades off the floor.
  4. Pause about one second at the top.
  5. Lower back down slowly.
  6. Repeat smoothly — no jerking.
- **Form cues:** Curl only until the shoulder blades clear the floor; lead with the breastbone, not the chin; keep the low back in floor contact.
- **Common mistakes:** Pulling on the head/neck; sitting all the way up; anchoring the feet and using hip flexors instead of abs.
- **Typical dosage:** 8–12 reps, 1–3 sets, 3+ days per week.
- **Progression:** Slow the lowering phase; hands by temples. | **Regression:** Smaller lift; hands reaching along the floor toward the knees.
- **Cautions:** Prefer the McGill curl-up if repeated flexion aggravates symptoms; keep the movement short and controlled.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — supine floor movement; the small trunk curl is foreshortened from a propped front camera.
- **Sources:** [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.low-back-pain-exercises.ad1482) (T1); [HealthLink BC](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/low-back-pain-exercises-reduce-pain) (T1); [StatPearls — Williams Back Exercises](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551558/) (T1)

### Side Plank
- **Aliases:** side bridge, lateral plank (Big Three #2)
- **Position:** side-lying
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** quadratus lumborum, obliques, lateral hip (gluteus medius)
- **Purpose:** Builds lateral core endurance that stabilizes the spine during walking and carrying, with minimal compressive load — a McGill Big Three staple echoed by Harvard, ACSM, and AAOS-style programs.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on one side with the forearm on the floor, elbow directly under the shoulder.
  2. Bend the knees to ~90 degrees (beginner version) or stack the legs straight (full version).
  3. Brace the trunk lightly.
  4. Lift the hips so the body forms a straight line from head to knees (or ankles).
  5. Hold, breathing steadily, hips stacked and not sagging.
  6. Lower with control and switch sides.
- **Form cues:** Shoulder, hip, and knee/ankle in one line; push the floor away through the forearm; keep the top hip stacked, not rolled back.
- **Common mistakes:** Hips sagging toward the floor; pelvis rotating backward; shoulder shrugged into the ear.
- **Typical dosage:** 10–20 second holds, 3–5 reps per side (or a 5-3-1 pyramid), most days.
- **Progression:** Straight-leg version; top leg raised; feet elevated. | **Regression:** Knees-bent version; hold from a raised surface.
- **Cautions:** Modify for painful shoulders/wrists; keep holds short and repeated rather than one long max hold.
- **Camera trackability:** trackable-hold — with the phone placed so the body is seen side-on (which is the natural framing for a side plank), the straight shoulder-hip-ankle line and hip height can be checked as a static pose; from a head-on/foot-on angle it degrades to not-trackable.
- **Sources:** [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/staying-healthy/three-moves-for-better-spine-health) (T1); [ACSM Health & Fitness Journal](https://journals.lww.com/acsm-healthfitness/fulltext/2013/03000/low_back_pain.4.aspx) (T2); [SquatUniversity](https://squatuniversity.com/2018/06/21/the-mcgill-big-3-for-core-stability/) (T3); [BackFitPro](https://www.backfitpro.com/mastering-the-mcgill-big-three-progressions-variations-and-common-pitfalls/) (T3)

### Front Plank
- **Aliases:** plank, forearm plank, prone bridge
- **Position:** prone
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** abdominals, back extensors, gluteals — whole-trunk stiffness
- **Purpose:** Trains the trunk to hold a rigid neutral line, building the endurance that protects the lower back during daily tasks. Included in AAOS's spine program and ACSM stabilization progressions.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie face down, then prop onto forearms with elbows under shoulders.
  2. Tuck the toes and brace the trunk.
  3. Lift the body so it forms a straight line from head to heels.
  4. Squeeze glutes and keep ribs gently drawn down.
  5. Hold while breathing normally.
  6. Lower the knees to rest.
- **Form cues:** One straight line — no sagging hips, no pike; push the floor away between the shoulder blades; look at the floor to keep the neck neutral.
- **Common mistakes:** Hips dropping and low back sagging; hips hiked high to make it easier; breath-holding.
- **Typical dosage:** 10–30 second holds, 2–5 reps, building total time gradually; 3+ days per week.
- **Progression:** Longer holds; leg lifts; hand plank with shoulder taps. | **Regression:** Knee plank; incline plank with forearms on a bench or wall.
- **Cautions:** Skip if it provokes back pain (a sagging plank compresses the lumbar spine); modify for shoulder issues.
- **Camera trackability:** trackable-hold — with the phone at floor level viewing the body side-on, the shoulder-hip-ankle straight-line angle is a clean static check; from directly in front (head-on) the body is foreshortened and it becomes not-trackable.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/recovery/spine-conditioning-program/) (T1); [ACSM Health & Fitness Journal](https://journals.lww.com/acsm-healthfitness/fulltext/2013/03000/low_back_pain.4.aspx) (T2); [NASM blog](https://blog.nasm.org/exercises-for-lower-back-pain) (T2)

### Dead Bug
- **Aliases:** deadbug, supine alternate arm-leg lowering
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** transverse abdominis, rectus abdominis, hip flexor control; anti-extension core
- **Purpose:** Teaches the core to keep the lower back quiet while arms and legs move — the same demand walking and lifting place on the spine, practiced from a safe, supported position.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back; lift legs to tabletop (hips and knees at 90 degrees).
  2. Reach both arms toward the ceiling.
  3. Gently flatten the low back toward the floor and brace lightly.
  4. Slowly lower one arm overhead and the opposite leg toward the floor.
  5. Stop before the low back arches off the floor; return to the start.
  6. Alternate sides with slow control.
- **Form cues:** Low back stays gently in floor contact the whole time; move slower than feels necessary; exhale as the limbs lower.
- **Common mistakes:** Letting the back arch as the leg extends; moving both limbs fast and losing the brace; holding the breath.
- **Typical dosage:** 6–10 reps per side, 2–3 sets, 3+ days per week.
- **Progression:** Straighter leg, lower hover; both legs (advanced); add light band tension. | **Regression:** Heel slides or single-limb movements only; smaller range.
- **Cautions:** Shrink the range if the back pops off the floor or hip flexors cramp.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — supine with limbs moving mostly along the camera's depth axis from a propped front view; reciprocal arm/leg angles need a side view.
- **Sources:** [NASM blog](https://blog.nasm.org/exercises-for-lower-back-pain) (T2); [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/exercise-fitness/dead-bug-exercise) (T3); [Back Intelligence](https://backintelligence.com/dead-bug-core-exercise/) (T3)

### Superman
- **Aliases:** prone back extension, prone arm/leg raise, full-body extension hold
- **Position:** prone
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** erector spinae, gluteals, hamstrings, posterior shoulder
- **Purpose:** Builds endurance in the back-extensor chain, which Cleveland Clinic highlights as protective against recurrent low back pain and slumped posture.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie face down with legs long and arms extended overhead (or by your sides for a lighter version).
  2. Gently brace the trunk.
  3. Lift the arms, chest, and legs a few centimeters off the floor together.
  4. Keep looking at the floor so the neck stays long.
  5. Hold briefly, then lower with control.
  6. Repeat.
- **Form cues:** Lift low and long — a few centimeters is enough; squeeze glutes to share the work; keep the neck neutral, gaze down.
- **Common mistakes:** Cranking the head and neck up; jerking into maximum height; holding the breath.
- **Typical dosage:** 2–5 second holds building toward 10–15 seconds, 8–12 reps, 2–3 sets, most days.
- **Progression:** Longer holds; alternate-limb "swimming"; light weights in hands (advanced). | **Regression:** Arms-only or legs-only lift; opposite arm/leg pairs (prone bird dog).
- **Cautions:** Skip or shorten range if extension provokes pain (e.g. sensitive facet joints or spinal stenosis-type symptoms — flexion-biased work suits those backs better).
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — prone floor exercise with a small vertical lift; invisible from a typical propped front camera.
- **Sources:** [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/got-back-pain-how-the-superman-exercise-can-help) (T1); [ACE exercise library](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/9/supermans/) (T2)

### Abdominal Draw-In / Bracing
- **Aliases:** abdominal contraction, transverse abdominis activation, drawing-in maneuver, core engagement
- **Position:** supine (also taught seated/standing/quadruped)
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** transverse abdominis, multifidus, pelvic floor (deep stabilizers)
- **Purpose:** Re-trains the deep corset muscles that stabilize the lumbar segments; the activation skill underlying every other stability exercise. ACSM stabilization programs start here, as does the AAOS abdominal contraction.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back with knees bent, feet flat, spine in its natural neutral curve.
  2. Breathe in normally; as you exhale, gently draw the lower belly in toward the spine.
  3. Keep the pelvis still — this is a muscle tension change, not a movement.
  4. Hold the light tension (about 30–50% effort) while breathing normally.
  5. Release fully.
  6. Repeat; later practice the same activation seated, standing, and on all fours.
- **Form cues:** Think "gently zip up below the belly button"; keep breathing — the ribs still move; effort stays light, not a maximal clench.
- **Common mistakes:** Sucking in with a big breath-hold; tilting the pelvis instead of just tensioning; over-bracing at full effort.
- **Typical dosage:** 2–5 second holds building to 10 seconds, 10 reps, 1–2 sets, daily.
- **Progression:** Add limb movement (heel slides, marching) while holding; carry the brace into bridges and bird dogs. | **Regression:** Practice with hands on the lower belly for feedback.
- **Cautions:** None significant; keep effort gentle.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — an internal muscle activation with virtually no visible joint motion from any camera angle.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/recovery/spine-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.low-back-pain-exercises.ad1482) (T1); [ACSM Health & Fitness Journal](https://journals.lww.com/acsm-healthfitness/fulltext/2013/03000/low_back_pain.4.aspx) (T2)

### Wall Sit
- **Aliases:** wall squat hold, wall slide-and-hold
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** wall
- **Target:** quadriceps, gluteals, with the low back supported flat against the wall
- **Purpose:** Strengthens the legs — the muscles that should power lifting and standing — while the wall keeps the lumbar spine supported and neutral, making it a back-friendly leg builder in Kaiser and HealthWise-based clinical programs.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand with your back against a wall, feet about 25–30 cm out from it, hip-width apart.
  2. Pull the belly in so the low back rests against the wall.
  3. Slide down the wall until the knees are moderately bent (well short of 90 degrees at first).
  4. Hold, keeping the low back in wall contact and breathing normally.
  5. Slide back up smoothly.
  6. Repeat.
- **Form cues:** Knees track over the middle of the feet; low back stays glued to the wall; weight through the heels.
- **Common mistakes:** Sliding too deep too soon; knees drifting inward; arching the back off the wall.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold at least 6 seconds per rep, 8–12 reps, working toward single holds up to 1 minute; 3+ days per week.
- **Progression:** Deeper knee bend (toward 90 degrees); longer holds. | **Regression:** Very shallow bend; shorter holds.
- **Cautions:** Keep the depth comfortable for the knees; come up if the legs shake excessively or the back leaves the wall.
- **Camera trackability:** trackable-hold — upright, front-facing static pose; knee-flexion angle and steady hip height are reliable front-camera signals for hold quality and duration.
- **Sources:** [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.low-back-pain-exercises.ad1482) (T1); [HealthLink BC](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/low-back-pain-exercises-reduce-pain) (T1)

### Bodyweight Squat
- **Aliases:** air squat, chair squat, sit-to-stand (regression), Williams squat
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** none | chair (regression)
- **Target:** quadriceps, gluteals, trunk stabilizers
- **Purpose:** Trains the hip-hinge-and-legs pattern that spares the back during lifting and everyday movement; the strengthening finisher of the Williams flexion series and a functional staple in trainer-led back programs.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart, toes slightly out.
  2. Brace the trunk lightly and keep the chest up.
  3. Bend hips and knees together, sitting back and down as if toward a chair.
  4. Descend to a comfortable depth with heels down.
  5. Push through the whole foot to stand tall.
  6. Repeat at a steady tempo.
- **Form cues:** Hips travel back as knees bend; spine stays long and neutral — hinge, don't slump; knees track over toes.
- **Common mistakes:** Rounding the low back at the bottom; heels lifting; knees collapsing inward.
- **Typical dosage:** 8–15 reps, 1–3 sets, 2–4 days per week.
- **Progression:** Slower tempo, deeper (comfortable) range, light dumbbell goblet hold. | **Regression:** Sit-to-stand from a chair; partial-depth squat holding a counter.
- **Cautions:** Limit depth if knees or back complain; quality of the neutral spine matters more than depth.
- **Camera trackability:** trackable-reps — ideal front-camera exercise: hip and knee flexion angles and vertical hip displacement give a strong, repeatable per-rep signal.
- **Sources:** [StatPearls — Williams Back Exercises](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK551558/) (T1); [Physiopedia — Williams Flexion Exercise](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Williams_Flexion_Exercise) (T2); [Exercise is Medicine / ACSM](https://www.exerciseismedicine.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/EIM_Rx-for-Health_Low-Back-Pain.pdf) (T2)

### Walking Program
- **Aliases:** aerobic walking, brisk walking for back health
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** whole-body aerobic conditioning; rhythmic, low-load spinal movement
- **Purpose:** Regular walking is one of the best-evidenced habits for easing and preventing non-specific low back pain — it nourishes spinal structures with gentle cyclical loading, builds stamina, and counters fear of movement.
- **Steps:**
  1. Start with a comfortable, level route and supportive shoes.
  2. Walk at an easy pace for 5–10 minutes to warm up.
  3. Build to a brisk pace where talking is possible but singing is not.
  4. Keep posture tall with relaxed arm swing.
  5. Accumulate your target minutes; slow gradually before stopping.
  6. Add a few gentle stretches afterward.
- **Form cues:** Tall posture, eyes ahead; relaxed shoulders and natural arm swing; land softly and roll through the foot.
- **Common mistakes:** Doing too much too soon after a flare; stopping all walking during minor soreness; hunching over a phone while walking.
- **Typical dosage:** Build toward 150 minutes/week of moderate aerobic activity (e.g. 30 minutes, 5 days), starting with bouts as short as 5–10 minutes.
- **Progression:** Longer/brisker walks, gentle hills. | **Regression:** Short frequent bouts; treadmill or pool walking.
- **Cautions:** If walking consistently increases leg pain, shorten bouts and seek guidance.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — locomotion leaves the camera frame; better tracked with step counting/motion sensors than a stationary front camera.
- **Sources:** [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/pain/home-remedies-for-low-back-pain) (T1); [AAOS OrthoInfo (warm-up guidance)](https://www.orthoinfo.org/en/recovery/spine-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Exercise is Medicine / ACSM](https://www.exerciseismedicine.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/EIM_Rx-for-Health_Low-Back-Pain.pdf) (T2); [ChoosePT / APTA](https://www.choosept.com/guide/physical-therapy-guide-low-back-pain) (T2)

## Extension/Flexion Protocol Movements
McKenzie-style extension movements below are typically prescribed when repeated backward bending eases or "centralizes" symptoms (leg pain retreating toward the spine). If any of them pushes symptoms further down a leg, that movement should be stopped. Williams-flexion counterparts (pelvic tilt, knee-to-chest, partial curl-up, hamstring stretch, hip flexor stretch, squat) are listed in the sections above.

### Prone Lying
- **Aliases:** face-down rest, McKenzie extension step 1
- **Position:** prone
- **Equipment:** none | towel (optional under hips)
- **Target:** lumbar spine (gentle passive extension), relaxation of guarding muscles
- **Purpose:** The entry point of the McKenzie extension progression: simply lying face down lets the lumbar spine settle into slight extension and often begins easing flexion-sensitive or disc-related symptoms.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie face down on a firm, comfortable surface.
  2. Rest the arms alongside the body and turn the head to one side.
  3. Let the whole back and hips relax completely.
  4. Breathe slowly and stay for a few minutes.
  5. Switch the head direction halfway through.
  6. Move on to prone-on-elbows when this feels easy.
- **Form cues:** Full relaxation is the exercise — let the belly sink into the surface; slow, easy breathing; no propping yet.
- **Common mistakes:** Staying tense through the back and shoulders; too short a stay to allow symptoms to settle; using a surface so soft the spine sags sideways.
- **Typical dosage:** 2–5 minutes, several times per day, especially during flare-ups (per McKenzie-based protocols).
- **Progression:** Prone on elbows, then prone press-ups. | **Regression:** Pillow under the abdomen to reduce the extension angle.
- **Cautions:** Discontinue if lying prone steadily increases pain or pushes symptoms further into a leg.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — motionless prone rest on the floor; nothing for pose landmarks to measure from a front camera.
- **Sources:** [StatPearls — McKenzie Back Exercises](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539720/) (T1); [Physiopedia — McKenzie Method](https://www.physio-pedia.com/McKenzie_Method) (T2)

### Prone on Elbows
- **Aliases:** sphinx pose, half press-up, forearm prop, prone prop
- **Position:** prone
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** lumbar extension mobility; light paraspinal activation
- **Purpose:** The intermediate McKenzie extension step: propping on the forearms adds a moderate, sustained backward bend that can reduce stiffness and centralize disc-related symptoms while staying gentle.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie face down, legs relaxed.
  2. Place forearms on the floor with elbows under (or slightly ahead of) the shoulders.
  3. Prop the upper body up onto the forearms.
  4. Let the hips and low back stay heavy and relaxed against the floor.
  5. Hold, breathing normally.
  6. Lower slowly back to flat.
- **Form cues:** Hips stay on the floor; let the low back "hang" into the arch rather than muscling it; shoulders down away from the ears.
- **Common mistakes:** Lifting the hips off the surface; shrugging and loading the neck; gripping the back muscles instead of relaxing into the prop.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 15–30 seconds (up to 2–3 minutes as a sustained prop), 2–4 reps, several times daily during a flare.
- **Progression:** Prone press-up on hands. | **Regression:** Return to flat prone lying; smaller prop with a pillow under the chest.
- **Cautions:** Stop if symptoms intensify or travel further down the leg; not the right direction for backs that feel worse with arching.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — prone floor posture; the modest chest elevation reads only from a side-on floor camera, not a propped front view.
- **Sources:** [StatPearls — McKenzie Back Exercises](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539720/) (T1); [Kaiser Permanente (press-up on forearms)](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.low-back-pain-exercises.ad1482) (T1); [Mayo Clinic Health System PDF](https://www.mayoclinichealthsystem.org/-/media/national-files/documents/hometown-health/2021/low-back-pain-exercises.pdf) (T1)

### Prone Press-Up
- **Aliases:** McKenzie press-up, extension in lying, cobra stretch, half push-up, back extension (NHS)
- **Position:** prone
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** lumbar extension mobility; anterior trunk/hip stretch
- **Purpose:** The full McKenzie extension movement: repeated press-ups take the lumbar spine through end-range backward bending, which in extension-responders relieves stiffness and centralizes leg symptoms. Also appears in NHS and Versus Arthritis routines as "back extensions"/"half push-ups."
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie face down with hands flat under the shoulders, elbows tucked.
  2. Keep hips, pelvis, and legs completely relaxed.
  3. Exhale and press through the hands, straightening the arms as far as comfortable.
  4. Let the back arch while the pelvis stays in contact with the floor.
  5. Pause briefly at the top.
  6. Lower slowly and repeat rhythmically.
- **Form cues:** Arms do the lifting — back and glutes stay relaxed; pelvis glued to the floor; exhale on the way up to let the spine sag into extension.
- **Common mistakes:** Hips lifting off the surface (turns it into a plank push-up); actively clenching back muscles; forcing height through pain.
- **Typical dosage:** 8–10 reps per set, repeated several times daily in McKenzie-based protocols; or hold 15–30 seconds for 2–4 reps as a stretch.
- **Progression:** Fuller elbow extension over sessions; brief end-range holds. | **Regression:** Prone on elbows; only partial arm straightening.
- **Cautions:** Not for backs that worsen with extension (e.g. stenosis-pattern or facet-irritable symptoms); stop if pain moves further down the leg.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — prone floor movement; upper-body elevation needs a side-on floor-level view, so a propped front camera cannot score reps reliably.
- **Sources:** [StatPearls — McKenzie Back Exercises](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539720/) (T1); [NHS Ayrshire & Arran (back extensions)](https://www.nhsaaa.net/musculoskeletal-msk-service-patient-portal/low-back-pain-msk-patient-portal/low-back-pain-exercises-msk-patient-portal/) (T1); [Versus Arthritis (half push-ups)](https://versusarthritis.org/about-arthritis/healthcare-professionals/training-and-education-resources/useful-resources/back-pain-exercises/) (T1); [Mayo Clinic Health System PDF](https://www.mayoclinichealthsystem.org/-/media/national-files/documents/hometown-health/2021/low-back-pain-exercises.pdf) (T1)

### Standing Back Extension
- **Aliases:** standing lumbar extension, extension in standing, hands-on-hips backward bend
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** lumbar extension mobility
- **Purpose:** A portable version of the extension protocol — useful as a "movement snack" to interrupt long sitting or bending-heavy tasks, and prescribed in McKenzie-based programs when lying down isn't practical.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand tall with feet shoulder-width apart.
  2. Place both hands on the lower back/upper buttocks, fingers pointing down or inward.
  3. Breathe out and lean the trunk backward over your hands as far as comfortable.
  4. Keep knees straight but not locked hard.
  5. Pause a second at the end point.
  6. Return upright and repeat.
- **Form cues:** Hands act as a fulcrum for the low back; move smoothly — no bouncing; keep the head in line with the trunk rather than dropping it back.
- **Common mistakes:** Bending the knees and swaying the hips forward instead of extending the spine; going dizzy by throwing the head back; rushing reps.
- **Typical dosage:** 8–10 reps, repeated every 1–2 hours during prolonged sitting/lifting days (McKenzie-style), or 1–2 sets daily.
- **Progression:** Slightly greater comfortable range over time. | **Regression:** Smaller lean; fingertips on a wall behind for confidence.
- **Cautions:** Ease off with dizziness or if extension worsens or peripheralizes symptoms.
- **Camera trackability:** trackable-reps — with a caveat: from a front camera the backward lean mostly appears as slight shoulder drop/trunk shortening (depth-axis motion), a weak signal; a side-on phone placement makes the trunk-extension angle clearly measurable.
- **Sources:** [StatPearls — McKenzie Back Exercises](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539720/) (T1); [Physiopedia — McKenzie Method](https://www.physio-pedia.com/McKenzie_Method) (T2); [East Lancashire Hospitals NHS](https://elht.nhs.uk/services/integrated-msk-pain-and-rheumatology-service/patient-information/spinal-pain/back-pain-general-mobility-exercises) (T1)

### Shoulder Blade Squeeze
- **Aliases:** scapular retraction
- **Position:** seated
- **Equipment:** chair
- **Target:** mid-back (rhomboids, lower trapezius); posture support
- **Purpose:** Mayo includes it in its 15-minute back routine to counter slumped posture, which indirectly unloads the lower back.
- **Steps:** 1. Sit tall on an armless chair. 2. Draw both shoulder blades back and together without shrugging. 3. Hold ~5 seconds breathing normally. 4. Release and repeat.
- **Typical dosage:** 5-second holds, 3–5 reps, twice daily.
- **Camera trackability:** trackable-reps (marginal) — seated front-facing; shoulder-width/chest-opening change is visible but subtle from a front camera.
- **Sources:** [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/adult-health/in-depth/back-pain/art-20546859) (T1)

### Seated QL (Quadratus Lumborum) Side Stretch
- **Aliases:** seated side-bend stretch, overhead reach flank stretch
- **Position:** seated
- **Equipment:** chair
- **Target:** quadratus lumborum, lateral trunk
- **Purpose:** Targets the deep side-of-spine muscle that commonly knots up with low back pain; taught by PT YouTubers as a desk-friendly release.
- **Steps:** 1. Sit tall, feet planted. 2. Anchor one hand on the seat. 3. Reach the other arm overhead and lean away until a flank stretch is felt. 4. Hold 20–30 seconds; swap sides.
- **Typical dosage:** 20–30 second holds, 2–3 per side, daily.
- **Camera trackability:** trackable-hold — seated lateral lean with overhead arm is clearly visible in the frontal plane (trunk-tilt and arm-elevation angles).
- **Sources:** [AskDoctorJo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/back-pain-relief-seated/) (T3); [Bob & Brad](https://www.bobandbrad.com/program/low-back-stretches) (T3) — no Tier 1/2 corroboration found in this pass.

### Positional Release (Strain–Counterstrain)
- **Aliases:** 90-second release, Jones counterstrain self-technique
- **Position:** supine/side-lying (position of maximum comfort)
- **Equipment:** none | towel/pillows
- **Purpose:** Bob & Brad popularize this ~90-second technique: fold the body gently around the sore spot into its most comfortable, shortened position, hold about 90 seconds, and return slowly — aiming to reset protective muscle tension.
- **Steps:** 1. Find the tender spot. 2. Position the body so the spot's tenderness drops markedly (usually shortening the muscle). 3. Rest there ~90 seconds, breathing slowly. 4. Return to neutral very slowly and re-test.
- **Typical dosage:** 90-second hold, 1–2 times per tender point, daily as needed.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — an idiosyncratic comfort position with no standard pose to verify.
- **Sources:** [Bob & Brad](https://www.bobandbrad.com/health-programs/back-pain-relief-program) (T3) — single-source (T3 only).

### Aquatic Exercise / Swimming
- **Aliases:** pool walking, hydrotherapy, water aerobics
- **Position:** standing (in water)
- **Equipment:** pool access
- **Target:** whole-body conditioning with buoyancy-reduced spinal load
- **Purpose:** Water supports body weight, letting people with irritable backs build aerobic fitness and strength with less compression; recommended in ACSM/Exercise is Medicine guidance among aerobic options.
- **Steps:** 1. Start with 10–15 minutes of easy pool walking or gentle swimming. 2. Keep strokes symmetrical and comfortable (backstroke/freestyle usually kinder than butterfly). 3. Build duration gradually toward 30 minutes.
- **Typical dosage:** 20–30 minutes, 2–3 times per week.
- **Camera trackability:** not-trackable — happens in a pool, outside any phone-camera setup.
- **Sources:** [Exercise is Medicine / ACSM](https://www.exerciseismedicine.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/EIM_Rx-for-Health_Low-Back-Pain.pdf) (T2) — single independent source in this pass.
