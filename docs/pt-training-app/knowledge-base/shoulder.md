Warm the shoulder first (5–10 min light activity or a warm shower). Stretch to gentle tension, never sharp pain.

## Stretches & Mobility

### Pendulum Swing
- **Aliases:** Codman exercise, pendulum stretch, arm dangle, shoulder circles (unsupported standing variant)
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** chair (or table for support)
- **Target:** glenohumeral joint capsule, rotator cuff (passive decompression), deltoid
- **Purpose:** Uses gravity and gentle momentum to move the shoulder joint without muscle effort, easing stiffness and pain. It is the classic warm-up/starting exercise for irritable, frozen, or post-injury shoulders.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand beside a table or sturdy chair and rest your unaffected hand on it.
  2. Hinge forward slightly at the hips and let the affected arm hang straight down, completely relaxed.
  3. Shift your body weight gently so the relaxed arm swings forward and back for 30–60 seconds.
  4. Repeat with side-to-side swings for 30–60 seconds.
  5. Then let the arm trace small circles (roughly dinner-plate size, ~30 cm), 10 circles each direction.
  6. Stand up slowly when finished.
- **Form cues:** Let the arm dangle like a rope — the swing comes from your body, not your shoulder muscles; keep the shoulder and hand loose; keep knees soft and back supported by your other arm.
- **Common mistakes:** Actively muscling the arm through the swing instead of letting it hang; making circles too large too soon; tensing the neck and shrugging while leaning over.
- **Typical dosage:** 30–60 seconds per direction (or ~10 circles each way), 1–2 sessions daily.
- **Progression:** Larger circle diameter as symptoms allow; add a light weight (1–2 kg) in the hand for more joint distraction. | **Regression:** Smaller swings, shorter time, more body support.
- **Cautions:** Keep swings small and pain-free; stop if you feel pinching, dizziness from bending forward, or increased pain afterward.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — user is hinged forward over a support with a small gravity-driven swing largely in the floor plane; landmark motion is subtle and the pose is partly side-on.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/rotator-cuff-and-shoulder-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/stretching-exercises-frozen-shoulder) (T1); [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/frozen-shoulder/multimedia/shoulder-exercises/img-20008121) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/rotator-cuff-tear-exercises-stretches) (T1); [NHS Ayrshire & Arran](https://www.nhsaaa.net/musculoskeletal-msk-service-patient-portal/shoulder-msk-patient-portal/shoulder-exercises-stiff-and-painful-shoulder-msk-patient-portal/) (T1); [UMass Memorial Health](https://www.ummhealth.org/health-library/exercises-for-shoulder-flexibility-pendulum-exercise) (T3)

### Cross-Body Arm Stretch
- **Aliases:** crossover arm stretch, cross-body reach, posterior shoulder stretch
- **Position:** standing | seated
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** posterior deltoid, posterior joint capsule, rotator cuff (infraspinatus/teres minor)
- **Purpose:** Stretches the back of the shoulder, which commonly tightens with shoulder pain and stiffness. Helps restore reach-across-the-body motion.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand or sit tall with relaxed shoulders.
  2. Bring the affected arm straight across the front of your chest.
  3. With the opposite hand, hold the affected arm above the elbow (not at the joint).
  4. Gently pull the arm closer to your chest until you feel a stretch in the back of the shoulder.
  5. Hold, then slowly release and lower the arm.
  6. Repeat on the other side if desired.
- **Form cues:** Keep the stretched arm roughly at chest height; keep both shoulders down away from the ears; pull with steady, gentle pressure.
- **Common mistakes:** Grabbing and pulling at the elbow joint itself; hiking the shoulder toward the ear; rotating the torso instead of moving the arm.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 15–30 seconds, 2–4 reps per side, daily.
- **Progression:** Slightly deeper pull or longer holds as tolerated. | **Regression:** Support the arm on a counter and lean gently instead of pulling.
- **Cautions:** Avoid if it causes pinching in the front of the shoulder; do not force the arm across if range is very limited — work within comfort.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-hold` — static pose is clearly visible waist-up from a front camera: wrist of stretched arm crossing the body midline with elbow near chest height (signal: wrist x-position vs. opposite shoulder, elbow height).
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/rotator-cuff-and-shoulder-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/stretching-exercises-frozen-shoulder) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/rotator-cuff-tear-exercises-stretches) (T1)

### Behind-Back Internal Rotation Stretch (Towel Stretch)
- **Aliases:** towel stretch, shoulder internal rotation stretch, hand-behind-back stretch, shoulder flexor stretch, passive internal rotation with stick
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** towel
- **Target:** anterior joint capsule, subscapularis, front-of-shoulder muscles; internal rotation range
- **Purpose:** Restores the reach-up-the-back motion (tucking in a shirt, fastening a bra) that is often lost first with frozen or stiff shoulders. The good arm provides the assisting force so the stiff shoulder can relax.
- **Steps:**
  1. Hold one end of a towel (about 1 m long) in your unaffected hand and raise that hand over your shoulder so the towel hangs down your back.
  2. Reach the affected hand behind your low back and grasp the bottom end of the towel, palm facing outward.
  3. Gently pull upward with the top (good) arm so the affected hand slides a little higher up your back.
  4. Stop when you feel a comfortable stretch in the front/back of the affected shoulder.
  5. Hold, then slowly lower and release.
  6. A stick or cane held behind the back can be used the same way.
- **Form cues:** Stand tall — no leaning or hunching; let the affected arm stay relaxed and be moved by the towel; keep the back of the hand tracking along the spine.
- **Common mistakes:** Yanking the towel quickly; arching the low back to fake extra range; shrugging the affected shoulder.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 15–30 seconds, repeat 2–4 times (some sources cycle 10–20 gentle pulls), daily.
- **Progression:** Grip the towel with hands closer together, or pull the hand slightly higher each session. | **Regression:** Start by simply resting the hand in a back pocket, or slide the hand up the back without a towel.
- **Cautions:** Avoid sharp front-of-shoulder pain or tingling in the hand; if range is very limited, keep the movement small and gentle.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — both hands and the towel are behind the torso and occluded from a front camera; movement amplitude is invisible.
- **Sources:** [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/stretching-exercises-frozen-shoulder) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/frozen-shoulder-exercises) (T1); [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/rotator-cuff-and-shoulder-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.rotator-cuff-exercises.ad1509) (T1); [ChoosePT/APTA](https://www.choosept.com/guide/physical-therapy-guide-frozen-shoulder-adhesive-capsulitis) (T2); [Mass General](https://www.massgeneral.org/assets/MGH/pdf/orthopaedics/sports-medicine/physical-therapy/mass-general-shoulder-stretching-exercises2.pdf) (T1)

### Wall Walk (Finger Walk)
- **Aliases:** finger walk, wall climbing, wall crawl, spider walk
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** wall
- **Target:** shoulder flexion/abduction range; deltoid, rotator cuff, joint capsule
- **Purpose:** Gradually restores overhead reach by letting the fingers and wall share the arm's weight. A staple early-mobility exercise for frozen shoulder and stiff, painful shoulders.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand facing a wall, about three-quarters of an arm's length away.
  2. Touch the wall at waist height with the fingertips of the affected arm, elbow slightly bent.
  3. Slowly "walk" your fingers up the wall, letting the fingers — not the shoulder muscles — do the work.
  4. Climb only as high as is comfortable.
  5. Pause briefly at the top, then walk the fingers back down (or lower the arm with help from the other hand).
  6. A side-facing version (standing with the affected shoulder toward the wall) trains abduction the same way.
- **Form cues:** Keep the shoulder relaxed and down as you climb; move slowly and rhythmically; stand tall rather than leaning into the wall.
- **Common mistakes:** Shrugging or hiking the shoulder to gain height; arching the back near the top; dropping the arm down quickly instead of lowering with control.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 climbs per session, 1–2 sessions daily (some sources suggest 10–20 repetitions per day).
- **Progression:** Stand slightly closer to the wall and climb higher; progress to a free-standing arm raise. | **Regression:** Climb a shorter distance; support the wrist with the other hand on the way down.
- **Cautions:** Stop at the height where pain begins; avoid forcing the last few centimeters; mild stretch is fine, sharp pain is not.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — with the phone propped at the wall facing the user, the climbing arm's elevation is clearly visible waist-up; primary signal: shoulder flexion angle (hip–shoulder–wrist) rising and falling each rep.
- **Sources:** [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/stretching-exercises-frozen-shoulder) (T1); [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/frozen-shoulder/multimedia/shoulder-exercises/img-20008121) (T1); [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.rotator-cuff-exercises.ad1509) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/frozen-shoulder-exercises) (T1)

### Doorway Chest Stretch
- **Aliases:** door lean, doorway pec stretch, corner stretch, wall press stretch (single-arm variant)
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** doorway
- **Target:** pectoralis major/minor, anterior shoulder capsule, biceps origin
- **Purpose:** Opens the front of the chest and shoulders, which tighten with prolonged sitting and rounded posture and can drag the shoulders forward into painful positions. Key stretch for posture-related shoulder/neck tension.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand in an open doorway.
  2. Place both forearms on the door frame, elbows bent about 90° and slightly above shoulder height.
  3. Step one foot forward through the doorway.
  4. Slowly shift your weight forward until you feel a stretch across the chest and front of the shoulders.
  5. Hold, breathing normally, then step back to release.
  6. Single-arm variant: place one palm/forearm on a wall or frame, elbow at 90°, and gently turn your body away from that arm.
- **Form cues:** Lead with the chest, not the chin; keep the core lightly braced so the low back does not arch; keep shoulders down away from the ears.
- **Common mistakes:** Lunging too far forward and straining the front of the shoulder; letting the head poke forward; elbows placed too high or too low on the frame.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 15–30 seconds, 2–4 reps, daily.
- **Progression:** Move the forearms higher on the frame or step further through. | **Regression:** Stretch one side at a time with a smaller lean; keep elbows at shoulder height.
- **Cautions:** Ease off if you feel pinching, numbness, or tingling down the arm; avoid aggressive stretching with a very irritable shoulder.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-hold` — front view from a phone placed beyond the doorway shows the goalpost arm position and forward lean (signals: elbow angle ~90°, wrists near frame, elbows at/above shoulder line); door frame may partly occlude landmarks, so framing guidance is needed.
- **Sources:** [NHS trusts (Leicestershire Partnership)](https://www.leicspart.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/475-Shoulder-exercises.pdf) (T1); [Versus Arthritis](https://versusarthritis.org/about-arthritis/exercising-with-arthritis/exercises-for-healthy-joints/exercises-for-the-shoulders/) (T1); [East Cheshire NHS](https://services.eastcheshire.nhs.uk/physiotherapy/self-help/shoulder-pain-physiotherapy) (T1); [NASM](https://blog.nasm.org/correcting-upper-crossed-syndrome) (T2); [AskDoctorJo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/video/top-3-shoulder-pain-relief-stretches/) (T3)

### Passive External Rotation with Stick
- **Aliases:** wand external rotation, cane-assisted external rotation, assisted ER stretch
- **Position:** standing | supine
- **Equipment:** none (household stick, cane, or umbrella)
- **Target:** external rotation range; subscapularis and anterior capsule (structures being stretched), infraspinatus/teres minor mobility
- **Purpose:** Restores outward rotation of the shoulder — needed for reaching behind the head and throwing motions — using the strong arm to push the stiff arm gently outward.
- **Steps:**
  1. Hold a light stick horizontally in front of you with both hands, elbows bent 90° and tucked at your sides.
  2. Keep the affected arm's elbow pressed lightly against your ribs.
  3. Using the good arm, push the stick sideways so the affected forearm rotates outward, away from your belly.
  4. Go until you feel a gentle stretch in the front of the shoulder.
  5. Hold, then return slowly to the start.
  6. Can also be done lying on your back with a small towel under the elbow.
- **Form cues:** Elbow stays glued to your side — only the forearm swings out; keep wrists straight; move slowly in both directions.
- **Common mistakes:** Letting the elbow drift away from the body (turns it into an arm swing); shrugging; bouncing at end range.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 15–30 seconds at end range, 2–4 reps, daily (or 10 slow repetitions).
- **Progression:** Slightly further rotation each week; progress toward the 90°-abducted position. | **Regression:** Smaller arc; perform lying down so the arm is fully supported.
- **Cautions:** Keep it gentle with a frozen shoulder — pushing hard into pain can flare symptoms; stop if sharp anterior pain occurs.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — standing front view shows the affected forearm sweeping laterally while the elbow stays pinned; primary signal: forearm angle (elbow–wrist vector) rotating relative to the torso midline, with elbow-to-hip distance as a form check.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/rotator-cuff-and-shoulder-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Mass General](https://www.massgeneral.org/assets/MGH/pdf/orthopaedics/sports-medicine/physical-therapy/mass-general-shoulder-stretching-exercises2.pdf) (T1); [Aurora Health Care](https://ahc.aurorahealthcare.org/fywb/x17468.pdf) (T1)

### Wand-Assisted Shoulder Flexion (Supine)
- **Aliases:** cane flexion AAROM, supine wand flexion, assisted overhead reach
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** none (stick/cane/wand)
- **Target:** shoulder flexion range; deltoid, joint capsule
- **Purpose:** Rebuilds overhead motion with the arm supported and gravity minimized, letting the strong arm guide the stiff or painful one. Common early exercise for frozen shoulder and general stiffness.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back with knees bent and feet flat.
  2. Hold a stick with both hands about shoulder-width apart, palms down (or thumbs up).
  3. Keeping elbows straight, slowly raise the stick up and overhead, the good arm guiding the affected one.
  4. Go as far overhead as comfortable, feeling a gentle stretch.
  5. Hold briefly at the top, then lower slowly back to your thighs.
- **Form cues:** Let the good arm do most of the work; keep ribs down and low back relaxed on the floor; move slowly and smoothly.
- **Common mistakes:** Arching the back to gain false range; bending the affected elbow; rushing the lowering phase.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 reps with a 10–20 second hold at end range, 2 sets, 1–2× daily.
- **Progression:** Perform seated or standing (more gravity work); reduce assistance from the good arm. | **Regression:** Smaller range; add a pillow under the upper arm.
- **Cautions:** Gentle assistance only — never crank the arm into pain; stop with any pinching at the top.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — supine on the floor with motion in the sagittal plane toward overhead; a propped front camera cannot capture the arc reliably.
- **Sources:** [Aurora Health Care](https://ahc.aurorahealthcare.org/fywb/x08332r26.pdf) (T1); [North Cheshire NHS](https://northcheshireandmersey.nhs.uk/media/luzaxp34/shoulder-range-of-motion-exercises-patient-advice-sheet.pdf) (T1); [Peak Physio](https://www.peak-physio.com.au/exercises/mobility-shoulder-flexion-assisted-rom-supine/) (T3)

### Sleeper Stretch
- **Aliases:** side-lying internal rotation stretch, posterior capsule stretch
- **Position:** side-lying
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** posterior capsule, infraspinatus/teres minor (internal rotation range)
- **Purpose:** Stretches the back of the shoulder capsule, which is often tight in people with shoulder pain, impingement-type symptoms, and throwers. Improves internal rotation.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on the side of the affected shoulder, body rolled slightly back (~30–45°) rather than straight on the point of the shoulder.
  2. Bring the bottom arm straight out from the shoulder, then bend the elbow 90° so the forearm points to the ceiling.
  3. With the top hand, grasp the back of the bottom wrist/forearm.
  4. Gently press the forearm down toward the floor (rotating the shoulder inward).
  5. Stop at a mild stretch in the back of the shoulder; hold.
  6. Release slowly and repeat.
- **Form cues:** You should feel it behind the shoulder, never in the front; keep the shoulder blade still — rotate the arm, not the whole shoulder; light pressure only.
- **Common mistakes:** Lying directly on the shoulder point (painful, blocks motion); pushing hard and fast; letting the elbow slide out of line with the shoulder.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 20–30 seconds, 2–4 reps, daily or before/after activity.
- **Progression:** Slightly greater rotation over weeks as tolerated. | **Regression:** Roll further onto the back; use the cross-body stretch instead, which is gentler on the same tissues.
- **Cautions:** Skip if it produces front-of-shoulder pinching or any nerve-like symptoms; this stretch should always feel mild.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — side-lying floor position with small forearm rotation; landmarks are stacked and partly occluded from a phone front camera.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/rotator-cuff-and-shoulder-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Reading Shoulder Unit](https://www.readingshoulderunit.com/upload/pdfs/stretching-exercises-updated-bih.pdf) (T1); [Physitrack](https://us.physitrack.com/home-exercise-video/sleeper-stretch) (T3); [Mike Reinold, PT](https://mikereinold.com/the-correct-way-to-perform-the-sleeper-stretch/) (T3)

### Armpit Stretch
- **Aliases:** counter stretch, shelf stretch
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** none (uses a counter, shelf, or chest-height surface)
- **Target:** inferior joint capsule, lats; overhead range
- **Purpose:** Gently opens the underside of the shoulder joint ("armpit" region), a capsule zone that stiffens markedly in frozen shoulder. Uses body weight via a knee bend instead of arm force.
- **Steps:**
  1. Using the good arm, lift the affected arm and rest it on a shelf or counter about chest height.
  2. Stand close, then slowly bend both knees a little, letting the armpit open and the shoulder stretch.
  3. Straighten the knees to release.
  4. With each repetition, bend the knees slightly deeper as comfort allows.
  5. Keep the arm resting passively on the surface throughout.
- **Form cues:** The knees create the stretch — the arm just rests; keep the back long rather than slumping; breathe out as you sink.
- **Common mistakes:** Forcing depth on the first rep; letting the arm slide off and catching it suddenly; shrugging the shoulder.
- **Typical dosage:** 10–20 gentle knee-bend repetitions, once daily.
- **Progression:** Deeper knee bend or a slightly higher shelf. | **Regression:** Lower surface, shallower bend.
- **Cautions:** Move slowly — this targets a sensitive capsule region; mild stretch only, and stop if pain lingers after the session.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — the movement is a small vertical body dip with the arm resting on furniture, typically viewed side-on and heavily occluded by the counter.
- **Sources:** [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/stretching-exercises-frozen-shoulder) (T1); [The Orthopedic Clinic](https://orthotoc.com/frozen-shoulder-exercises-restore-mobility/) (T3); [JAG PT](https://www.jagpt.com/blog/physical-therapy-exercises-for-frozen-shoulder/) (T3)

### Overhead Reach Stretch
- **Aliases:** overhead stretch, arms-overhead stretch, elevation stretch
- **Position:** standing | seated | supine
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** shoulder flexion range; lats, pecs, upper back
- **Purpose:** A simple whole-shoulder stretch that restores comfortable overhead reach and counters the compressed, rounded position of desk work.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand or sit tall; interlace fingers or keep hands separate.
  2. Keeping elbows straight, slowly raise both arms up over your head.
  3. Reach until you feel a stretch through the shoulders, chest, and upper back.
  4. Hold, breathing steadily.
  5. Lower the arms slowly back to your sides.
- **Form cues:** Keep ribs down (don't flare the chest and arch the low back); reach long through the fingertips; keep the neck relaxed.
- **Common mistakes:** Arching the lumbar spine to fake overhead range; shrugging into the ears; holding the breath.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 15–30 seconds, 2–4 reps, daily.
- **Progression:** Add a gentle side-lean at the top; hold a light stick/towel between the hands. | **Regression:** Raise one arm at a time or reach only to eye level; do it supine.
- **Cautions:** Stay below the height where pinching starts; an irritable shoulder may prefer the supine, supported version.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-hold` — front camera clearly sees both wrists above head level, elbows extended (signals: wrist y above nose/head keypoint, elbow angle ~180°, shoulder flexion angle hip–shoulder–wrist).
- **Sources:** [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.shoulder-stretches-exercises.ad1512) (T1); [NAC Ortho / Healthwise care instructions](https://nacortho.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/shoulder-exercises.pdf) (T3); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/rotator-cuff-tear-exercises-stretches) (T1)

### Overhead Pulley
- **Aliases:** shoulder pulley, rope-and-pulley exercise
- **Position:** seated | standing
- **Equipment:** doorway (over-door pulley kit)
- **Target:** shoulder flexion/abduction range; assisted mobility for rotator cuff and capsule
- **Purpose:** The strong arm powers the pulley so the stiff or painful arm gets smooth, pain-managed passive elevation. Widely used for frozen shoulder and post-injury stiffness.
- **Steps:**
  1. Hang the pulley over a door and sit or stand facing (or with your back to) the door as instructed by the kit.
  2. Hold one handle in each hand.
  3. Pull down with the good arm so the affected arm rises up in front of you.
  4. Raise it as high as comfortable; hold the top position 5–15 seconds.
  5. Slowly let the affected arm come back down with control.
  6. The same setup can assist reaching behind the back (hand-behind-back rotation).
- **Form cues:** Sit tall — don't lean or hunch to gain height; let the affected arm stay relaxed; keep the motion slow and even.
- **Common mistakes:** Shrugging the affected shoulder as it rises; jerky pulls; slumping sideways.
- **Typical dosage:** 10–20 repetitions per direction, 1–2 sessions daily; holds of 15–20 seconds at end range.
- **Progression:** Higher end range, longer holds, add behind-back direction. | **Regression:** Smaller arc, sit closer to the door.
- **Cautions:** Height should build gradually — forcing range can flare a frozen shoulder; stop with sharp pain or pins-and-needles.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — with the phone propped near the door facing the seated user, the affected arm's rise/fall is clear waist-up; primary signal: shoulder elevation angle (hip–shoulder–wrist) cycling each rep.
- **Sources:** [ChoosePT/APTA](https://www.choosept.com/guide/physical-therapy-guide-frozen-shoulder-adhesive-capsulitis) (T2); [Physitrack](https://us.physitrack.com/home-exercise-video/ghjt-ir-with-pulley-(hand-behind-back)) (T3); [Choose PT 1st](https://www.choosept1st.com/blog/shoulder-pulley-exercises-for-home-a-physical-therapist-s-step-by-step-guide-in-2026) (T3)

### Upper Trapezius Stretch
- **Aliases:** upper trap stretch, ear-to-shoulder neck stretch, lateral neck stretch
- **Position:** seated | standing
- **Equipment:** chair
- **Target:** upper trapezius, lateral neck muscles
- **Purpose:** Releases the band of muscle between neck and shoulder that tightens with stress, desk posture, and shoulder guarding — a major driver of posture-related shoulder/neck tension.
- **Steps:**
  1. Sit tall on a chair; anchor the side you want to stretch by sitting on that hand or gripping the chair seat.
  2. Tilt your head sideways, taking the opposite ear toward the opposite shoulder.
  3. Stop when you feel a stretch along the side of the neck into the top of the shoulder.
  4. Optionally rest the free hand lightly on the head for a small amount of extra weight (no pulling).
  5. Hold, then return to neutral slowly and switch sides.
- **Form cues:** Keep the anchored shoulder pressed down — if it rides up, the stretch disappears; tilt straight sideways, nose forward; gentle overpressure only.
- **Common mistakes:** Pulling hard on the head; rotating the head instead of tilting; letting the shoulder shrug and follow the head.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 15–30 seconds, 2–3 reps per side, 1–2× daily.
- **Progression:** Slight head rotation variations to bias different fibers; add gentle hand overpressure. | **Regression:** Smaller tilt, no overpressure.
- **Cautions:** Keep it pain-free — no nerve-like tingling down the arm; ease off if it triggers headache or dizziness.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-hold` — head lateral tilt is easily measured from a front camera (signal: ear–shoulder distance asymmetry / head roll angle vs. shoulder line) with a hold timer; upper-body-only framing suffices.
- **Sources:** [WWL NHS neck & upper back leaflet](https://www.wwl.nhs.uk/media/.leaflets/6214d1fe0a5965.24837304.pdf) (T1); [Summit Orthopedics](https://www.summitortho.com/find-care/services-conditions/back-neck-spine/treatments/spine-exercises-library/upper-trap-stretch/) (T3); [AskDoctorJo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/video/top-3-shoulder-pain-relief-stretches/) (T3); [Physitrack](https://www.physitrack.com/exercise-library/how-to-perform-the-upper-trap-stretch-exercise) (T3)

### Shoulder Rolls
- **Aliases:** shoulder circles, shoulder rotations
- **Position:** standing | seated
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** upper trapezius, scapular muscles, general shoulder-girdle circulation
- **Purpose:** A gentle warm-up and tension-release movement that lubricates the shoulder girdle and interrupts static desk posture. Good entry point on painful days.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand or sit tall with arms relaxed at your sides.
  2. Lift both shoulders up toward your ears.
  3. Roll them backward and down in a smooth circle.
  4. Complete about 5 circles backward.
  5. Reverse and roll forward for 5 circles.
- **Form cues:** Make the circles slow and as large as comfortable; keep arms heavy and relaxed; finish each backward roll with the shoulder blades settling down and back.
- **Common mistakes:** Tiny fast shrug-like circles; poking the chin forward; holding the breath.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 circles each direction, several times a day (especially during desk breaks).
- **Progression:** Larger, slower circles; combine with breathing (inhale up, exhale down-and-back). | **Regression:** One shoulder at a time; smaller range.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — cyclic vertical/circular displacement of both shoulder keypoints relative to the ears is detectable from a front camera, though the amplitude is modest; primary signal: shoulder-keypoint y-oscillation (shoulder–ear distance cycling).
- **Cautions:** Keep circles within a comfortable range; skip the forward direction if it provokes pinching.
- **Sources:** [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/posture-exercises) (T1); [AskDoctorJo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/video/5-sore-shoulder-stretches/) (T3); [Mayo Clinic Health System](https://www.mayoclinichealthsystem.org/hometown-health/speaking-of-health/dont-just-sit-there-exercises-for-the-office) (T1)

## Strengthening

### Isometric Shoulder External Rotation
- **Aliases:** static external rotation, door-frame press-out, ER wall push
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** wall (or doorway)
- **Target:** infraspinatus, teres minor (posterior rotator cuff)
- **Purpose:** Builds rotator cuff activation without any joint movement, so it can be started when motion is painful. Often used early in rotator cuff and impingement rehab to reduce pain sensitivity.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand beside a wall corner or in a doorway, affected arm nearest the frame.
  2. Bend the elbow 90°, upper arm resting at your side, thumb up.
  3. Place the back of the hand/outer wrist against the wall or frame.
  4. Press outward into the wall as if rotating the forearm away from your body — the arm should not move.
  5. Build effort gradually over ~3 seconds, hold a comfortable contraction 5–10 seconds, then ease off over ~3 seconds.
  6. Rest and repeat.
- **Form cues:** Start around 20–30% effort and build only as comfort allows; keep the elbow pinned at your side; shoulders stay down and relaxed.
- **Common mistakes:** Maximal effort straight away; shrugging while pressing; letting the elbow drift from the body.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 second holds × 10 reps, 1–2× daily.
- **Progression:** Increase effort toward ~70–80%, longer holds, then transition to band external rotation. | **Regression:** Lighter effort, shorter holds.
- **Cautions:** Effort level should never spike pain above mild; ease off if pain lingers afterward.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — by design nothing moves; the pressing force and its direction are invisible to pose landmarks.
- **Sources:** [East Cheshire NHS](https://services.eastcheshire.nhs.uk/physiotherapy/self-help/shoulder-pain-physiotherapy) (T1); [OrthoIndy](https://www.orthoindy.com/wp-content/uploads/Shoulder-Isometric-Strengthening-Program.pdf) (T3); [Physitrack](https://us.physitrack.com/home-exercise-video/isometric-shoulder-external-rotation-against-wall) (T3)

### Isometric Shoulder Internal Rotation
- **Aliases:** static internal rotation, IR wall/door-frame press
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** wall (or doorway)
- **Target:** subscapularis, pectoralis major
- **Purpose:** Activates the front rotator cuff without joint motion — a pain-tolerant starting point when moving the shoulder is aggravating.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand at a wall corner or doorway with the affected arm nearest the frame.
  2. Bend the elbow 90°, upper arm at your side.
  3. Place the palm/inner wrist against the frame.
  4. Press inward, as if rotating the forearm toward your belly; nothing should move.
  5. Ramp up gently, hold 5–10 seconds, ramp down.
  6. Rest and repeat.
- **Form cues:** Elbow stays tucked to the ribs; gradual build and release of effort; breathe normally through the hold.
- **Common mistakes:** Pushing with the whole body; wrist bending under load; jumping straight to maximum effort.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 second holds × 10 reps, 1–2× daily.
- **Progression:** Higher effort, longer holds, progress to band internal rotation. | **Regression:** Press into your own opposite hand at a very light effort.
- **Cautions:** Mild effort first; stop if front-of-shoulder pain builds.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — static contraction with no visible landmark motion; force direction cannot be inferred by camera.
- **Sources:** [OrthoIndy](https://www.orthoindy.com/wp-content/uploads/Shoulder-Isometric-Strengthening-Program.pdf) (T3); [East Cheshire NHS](https://services.eastcheshire.nhs.uk/physiotherapy/self-help/shoulder-pain-physiotherapy) (T1); [Liv Hospital](https://int.livhospital.com/essential-how-to-do-isometric-shoulder-exercises-safely/) (T3)

### Isometric Shoulder Abduction (Wall Press)
- **Aliases:** static abduction, sideways wall push; related: isometric flexion (fist press into wall)
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** wall, towel
- **Target:** supraspinatus, deltoid
- **Purpose:** Strengthens the "lift-the-arm-sideways" muscles with zero movement, useful when active lifting is painful (e.g., rotator cuff irritation).
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand side-on to a wall, affected arm against it, elbow bent 90°.
  2. Place a folded towel between the outside of the arm and the wall.
  3. Push the arm sideways into the wall as if lifting it away from your body — the arm does not move.
  4. Build effort gradually (start ~20%), hold 5–10 seconds, then relax.
  5. Repeat. Flexion variant: face the wall, make a soft fist with thumb up, and press the fist gently forward into the wall for ~6 seconds.
- **Form cues:** Keep the trunk vertical — don't lean into the wall; shoulder stays down; smooth ramp up, ramp down.
- **Common mistakes:** Leaning the whole body instead of pressing with the shoulder; shrugging; holding the breath.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 second holds × 10 reps, 1–2× daily; effort progressing from ~20% toward ~80% over sessions.
- **Progression:** More effort, then dynamic scaption/lateral raises with light weight. | **Regression:** Lighter pressure, shorter holds.
- **Cautions:** Keep effort below pain threshold; a mild ache that settles quickly is acceptable, sharp pain is not.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — static press with no joint-angle change; only body-lean errors would be visible, not the exercise itself.
- **Sources:** [OrthoIndy](https://www.orthoindy.com/wp-content/uploads/Shoulder-Isometric-Strengthening-Program.pdf) (T3); [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.rotator-cuff-exercises.ad1509) (T1); [Liv Hospital](https://int.livhospital.com/essential-how-to-do-isometric-shoulder-exercises-safely/) (T3)

### Band External Rotation (Elbow at Side)
- **Aliases:** resisted external rotation, outward rotation with band
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** resistance band, towel (optional, under elbow)
- **Target:** infraspinatus, teres minor
- **Purpose:** The cornerstone rotator cuff strengthener. Builds the posterior cuff that stabilizes the ball in the socket, protecting against impingement and easing everyday reaching pain.
- **Steps:**
  1. Anchor a band at elbow height (door anchor or doorknob loop).
  2. Stand side-on with the affected arm farther from the anchor; hold the band, elbow bent 90° and tucked at your side.
  3. Start with the hand near your belly.
  4. Keeping the elbow pinned, rotate the forearm outward away from your body a comfortable distance.
  5. Pause briefly, then return slowly with control.
  6. Repeat; a small towel between elbow and ribs helps keep position.
- **Form cues:** Elbow glued to the side the whole set; move slowly, especially on the return; keep wrist straight and shoulder blades gently set back and down.
- **Common mistakes:** Elbow drifting away from the body; using trunk rotation for momentum; letting the band snap back quickly.
- **Typical dosage:** 3 sets of 8 reps building to 3 × 12–15, about 3 days/week; some protocols add a 5-second hold per rep.
- **Progression:** Thicker band, more range, or the 90°-abducted version. | **Regression:** Lighter band or isometric ER instead.
- **Cautions:** Choose a resistance that leaves the set challenging but pain-free; stop if pain exceeds a mild level or lingers next day.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — front view shows the forearm sweeping outward while the elbow stays pinned; primary signal: elbow–wrist vector angle relative to torso midline cycling each rep (elbow-to-hip distance flags the "elbow drift" fault). Band tension itself is invisible, but the motion is clear.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/rotator-cuff-and-shoulder-conditioning-program/) (T1); [ChoosePT/APTA](https://www.choosept.com/guide/physical-therapy-guide-frozen-shoulder-adhesive-capsulitis) (T2); [Physiopedia](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Therapeutic_Exercise_for_the_Shoulder) (T2)

### Band Internal Rotation (Elbow at Side)
- **Aliases:** resisted internal rotation, inward rotation with band
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** resistance band
- **Target:** subscapularis, pectoralis major
- **Purpose:** Strengthens the front of the rotator cuff, balancing the shoulder's rotation strength and supporting the joint during pushing and reaching-across tasks.
- **Steps:**
  1. Anchor a band at elbow height.
  2. Stand side-on with the affected arm nearest the anchor; hold the band with the elbow bent 90° at your side.
  3. Start with the forearm pointing toward the anchor.
  4. Rotate the forearm inward across your belly, elbow staying pinned.
  5. Pause, then return slowly against the band's pull.
  6. Repeat.
- **Form cues:** Only the forearm moves; stand tall without twisting the trunk; control the return phase fully.
- **Common mistakes:** Twisting the torso for extra pull; elbow lifting or drifting; rushing reps.
- **Typical dosage:** 3 sets of 8 building to 3 × 12–15, ~3 days/week.
- **Progression:** Thicker band; slower eccentric. | **Regression:** Lighter band; isometric IR.
- **Cautions:** Keep resistance modest — the cuff responds to controlled, light-to-moderate loading; avoid pain above mild.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — mirror of band ER: forearm sweeps across the belly from a clearly visible start; primary signal: elbow–wrist vector angle vs. torso midline, wrist crossing the midline each rep.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/rotator-cuff-and-shoulder-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.rotator-cuff-exercises.ad1509) (T1)

### External Rotation at 90° Abduction (Band)
- **Aliases:** ER at 90/90, high external rotation, throwing-position rotation
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** resistance band
- **Target:** infraspinatus, teres minor, scapular stabilizers
- **Purpose:** Trains the rotator cuff in the elevated, "throwing" position used for overhead sport and reaching to high shelves. A later-stage progression once low-position rotation is strong and painless.
- **Steps:**
  1. Anchor a band at shoulder height.
  2. Face the anchor; raise the affected arm out to the side to shoulder height with the elbow bent 90°, forearm parallel to the floor holding the band.
  3. Keeping the upper arm level, rotate the forearm up and back until it points toward the ceiling.
  4. Pause briefly.
  5. Lower the forearm slowly back to parallel.
  6. Repeat without letting the elbow drop.
- **Form cues:** Elbow stays level with the shoulder throughout; shoulder blade set gently down and back; smooth tempo both directions.
- **Common mistakes:** Elbow sagging below shoulder line; arching the back at the top; using momentum.
- **Typical dosage:** 3 sets of 8 building to 3 × 12, ~3 days/week.
- **Progression:** Thicker band, slower eccentrics. | **Regression:** Return to elbow-at-side external rotation.
- **Cautions:** Skip while symptoms are irritable — this position is more demanding; stop with any pinching at the top.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — front view shows the forearm rotating from horizontal to vertical at shoulder height; primary signal: elbow angle position pair — shoulder abduction held ~90° (hip–shoulder–elbow) while the elbow–wrist vector swings ~90° each rep.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/rotator-cuff-and-shoulder-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Physiopedia](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Therapeutic_Exercise_for_the_Shoulder) (T2)

### Side-Lying External Rotation (Dumbbell)
- **Aliases:** lying dumbbell external rotation, side-lying ER
- **Position:** side-lying
- **Equipment:** light dumbbell, towel (roll between elbow and ribs)
- **Target:** infraspinatus, teres minor
- **Purpose:** Isolates the posterior rotator cuff with gravity as clean resistance — one of the highest-value cuff strengtheners, common in tendinopathy programs.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on the unaffected side, head supported.
  2. Bend the top (affected) elbow to 90°, upper arm resting along your side; place a small towel roll between elbow and ribs.
  3. Hold a light dumbbell with the forearm resting across your stomach.
  4. Keeping the elbow fixed, rotate the forearm up toward the ceiling as far as comfortable.
  5. Pause, then lower slowly back across the stomach.
  6. Repeat, then switch sides if training both.
- **Form cues:** Elbow pinned against the towel throughout; rotate only the forearm — the trunk stays still; slow on the way down.
- **Common mistakes:** Rolling the trunk backward to lift higher; too much weight (the cuff needs light loads); dropping the weight quickly.
- **Typical dosage:** 2–3 sets of 10–15 reps with a very light weight (0.5–2 kg to start), ~3 days/week.
- **Progression:** Small weight increments; slower eccentric phase. | **Regression:** No weight, or band ER standing.
- **Cautions:** Light and controlled beats heavy; stop if the top of the shoulder pinches or aches sharply.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — side-lying floor position; the rotating forearm moves mostly in a plane the front camera cannot resolve and landmarks are stacked/occluded.
- **Sources:** [MOON Shoulder Group PT guideline](https://moonshoulder.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/Nonoperative-Treatment-of-Rotator-Cuff-Tendinopathy-PT-Guidelines.pdf) (T1); [StrengthLog](https://www.strengthlog.com/lying-dumbbell-external-shoulder-rotation/) (T3); [Rehab Hero](https://www.rehabhero.ca/exercise/side-lying-shoulder-external-rotations) (T3)

### Standing Row (Band)
- **Aliases:** elastic band row, resisted row, seated row (chair variant)
- **Position:** standing | seated
- **Equipment:** resistance band
- **Target:** middle and lower trapezius, rhomboids, posterior deltoid, biceps
- **Purpose:** Strengthens the mid-back muscles that anchor the shoulder blade, sharing load with the rotator cuff and pulling the shoulders out of a rounded, painful posture.
- **Steps:**
  1. Anchor a band at elbow height (loop around a doorknob or door anchor).
  2. Stand facing the anchor, arm(s) extended forward holding the band with light tension.
  3. Pull the hand(s) back toward your ribs, elbow(s) sliding close past the body.
  4. As you pull, squeeze the shoulder blades together and down.
  5. Pause 1–2 seconds at the back.
  6. Return slowly to the start and repeat.
- **Form cues:** Lead with the shoulder blade, then the elbow; keep shoulders away from the ears; stand tall — no leaning back.
- **Common mistakes:** Shrugging during the pull; leaning the trunk backward for momentum; letting the band yank the arm forward on the return.
- **Typical dosage:** 3 sets of 8 building to 3 × 12–15, ~3 days/week.
- **Progression:** Thicker band, single-arm rows, or add a pause. | **Regression:** Lighter band, seated with elbow supported.
- **Cautions:** Keep range comfortable; stop if pinching occurs at the front of the shoulder.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — the rowing motion travels mainly along the camera's depth axis (hand toward the torso), and the user faces the band anchor, making joint-angle change poorly visible from a phone front camera.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/rotator-cuff-and-shoulder-conditioning-program/) (T1); [NASM](https://blog.nasm.org/correcting-upper-crossed-syndrome) (T2); [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.rotator-cuff-exercises.ad1509) (T1)

### Scaption (Full-Can Raise)
- **Aliases:** scapular-plane raise, full can exercise, thumbs-up raise
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** light dumbbell (or none)
- **Target:** supraspinatus, deltoid, scapular upward rotators
- **Purpose:** Strengthens the arm-raising muscles in the shoulder's natural "scapular plane" (about 30–45° forward of sideways) with thumbs up, which gives the rotator cuff maximum room and is gentler than a strict lateral raise.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand tall holding light dumbbells (or empty hands), arms at your sides.
  2. Turn thumbs up (like holding full cans you don't want to spill).
  3. Raise both arms up and slightly forward — about 30–45° in front of your sides — keeping elbows straight.
  4. Stop at shoulder height.
  5. Pause briefly.
  6. Lower slowly with control and repeat.
- **Form cues:** Thumbs stay pointing up the whole rep; stop at shoulder height — no higher; no swinging or trunk lean.
- **Common mistakes:** Shrugging as the arms rise; lifting above shoulder height into a pinchy zone; using momentum from the hips.
- **Typical dosage:** 2–3 sets of 8–15 reps with light weight, ~3 days/week.
- **Progression:** Small weight increases; brief holds at the top. | **Regression:** No weight; smaller range (to 45–60° elevation).
- **Cautions:** Keep it below the painful arc — if a segment of the lift pinches, shorten the range; light loads only for the cuff.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — the bilateral arm raise to shoulder height is highly visible waist-up from the front; primary signal: shoulder elevation/abduction angle (hip–shoulder–wrist) cycling 0°→~90°, with wrist-above-shoulder as an overshoot flag.
- **Sources:** [MOON Shoulder Group PT guideline](https://moonshoulder.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/Nonoperative-Treatment-of-Rotator-Cuff-Tendinopathy-PT-Guidelines.pdf) (T1); [Rehab Hero](https://www.rehabhero.ca/exercise/full-can-raises) (T3); [Movement Redefined](https://movementredefinedaz.com/exercises-library/scaption-raises-strengthen-your-shoulders-in-a-safer-plane/) (T3)

### Bent-Over Horizontal Abduction (Reverse Fly)
- **Aliases:** bent-over lateral raise, reverse fly, prone horizontal abduction (table variant)
- **Position:** standing (hip hinge) | prone
- **Equipment:** light dumbbell
- **Target:** posterior deltoid, middle/lower trapezius, infraspinatus, teres minor
- **Purpose:** Strengthens the back of the shoulder and mid-back together, improving scapular control and balancing the front-dominant strength pattern common in shoulder pain.
- **Steps:**
  1. Hinge forward at the hips (chest toward parallel with the floor), free hand supported on a table or thigh — or lie face down on a bed/bench with the arm hanging.
  2. Let the affected arm hang straight down holding a light weight, thumb rotated slightly forward/up.
  3. Keeping the elbow nearly straight, raise the arm out to the side to shoulder level.
  4. Squeeze the shoulder blade toward the spine at the top.
  5. Pause 1–2 seconds.
  6. Lower slowly and repeat.
- **Form cues:** Keep the neck long and back flat while hinged; lead with the shoulder blade, not the hand; stop at shoulder height.
- **Common mistakes:** Swinging the weight; rounding the back; shrugging into the neck.
- **Typical dosage:** 3 sets of 8 building to 3 × 12 with light weight (max ~2 kg to start), ~3 days/week.
- **Progression:** 0.5 kg increments; pause reps. | **Regression:** No weight, or support the chest prone on a bench.
- **Cautions:** Keep loads light; skip if the hinged position aggravates the low back — use the prone-supported version instead.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — the trunk is hinged toward the camera (or prone), so the arm's sideways arc is foreshortened and the head-down posture defeats reliable front-view landmarking.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/rotator-cuff-and-shoulder-conditioning-program/) (T1); [PMC study on prone horizontal abduction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4305608/) (T1 literature); [Rehab Hero](https://www.rehabhero.ca/exercise/prone-t-raise) (T3)

### Prone Y-T-W Raises
- **Aliases:** I-Y-T raises, prone Y raise / T raise / W raise, lower-trap raises
- **Position:** prone
- **Equipment:** none (optional light dumbbells; towel under forehead)
- **Target:** lower and middle trapezius, rhomboids, posterior deltoid, rotator cuff
- **Purpose:** A scapular-stabilizer series that strengthens the muscles that rotate and anchor the shoulder blade — key for healthy overhead motion and relief of impingement-type and postural shoulder pain.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie face down with forehead on a rolled towel.
  2. Y: reach arms overhead at ~30–45° from the body, thumbs up; lift both arms a few centimeters off the floor, squeezing the lower shoulder blades; hold 1–2 seconds, lower.
  3. T: arms straight out to the sides at shoulder level, thumbs up; lift, squeeze between the blades, lower.
  4. W: elbows bent, drawn down toward the back pockets so arms form a W; lift and squeeze, lower.
  5. Keep the neck neutral (nose toward the floor) throughout.
  6. Complete the set for each letter position.
- **Form cues:** Lift comes from the shoulder blades, not from arching the neck or low back; thumbs point to the ceiling; small lifts done well beat big lifts done poorly.
- **Common mistakes:** Lifting the head/chest to fake arm height; shrugging toward the ears (especially in Y); rushing between positions.
- **Typical dosage:** 10 reps per letter, 2–3 rounds, once daily or ~3 days/week.
- **Progression:** Add 0.5–1 kg dumbbells or perform over an exercise ball; add holds. | **Regression:** Do one letter per session; slide arms along the floor without lifting.
- **Cautions:** Keep lifts small and pain-free; skip positions that pinch and return to them later.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — prone on the floor; a front camera cannot see lift amplitude (a few centimeters vertical, toward/away from the floor plane).
- **Sources:** [PMC systematic review of scapular stabilizer exercises](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4886800/) (T1 literature); [Hinge Health](https://www.hingehealth.com/resources/articles/scapular-stabilization-exercises/) (T3); [Rehab Hero](https://www.rehabhero.ca/exercise/prone-t-raise) (T3); [Maristow Chiropractic Y-W-T handout](https://maristowchiropractic.com/uploads/exercises/ywt.pdf) (T3)

### Push-Up Plus (Wall / Knee / Full)
- **Aliases:** scapular push-up, wall push-up plus, serratus push-up, plus push-up
- **Position:** standing (wall) | quadruped | prone (plank)
- **Equipment:** wall (easiest variant) or none
- **Target:** serratus anterior, pectorals, triceps, scapular stabilizers
- **Purpose:** The "plus" — pushing the upper back toward the ceiling at the top — is one of the best activators of the serratus anterior, the muscle that keeps the shoulder blade flush against the ribcage for smooth, pain-free overhead motion.
- **Steps:**
  1. Wall version: stand arm's length from a wall, palms on the wall at shoulder height/width.
  2. Bend the elbows to bring your chest toward the wall (a wall push-up).
  3. Push back until elbows are straight.
  4. At the top, keep pushing: spread the shoulder blades apart so the upper back rounds slightly — that extra push is the "plus."
  5. Hold the plus 1–2 seconds, then let the blades settle together and repeat.
  6. Progress to hands-and-knees, then full plank position for the same sequence.
- **Form cues:** Elbows straight during the plus — the movement is only the shoulder blades; keep the trunk in one line (no sagging hips in plank versions); slow and deliberate.
- **Common mistakes:** Bending the elbows during the plus phase; poking the head forward; sagging or piking at the hips.
- **Typical dosage:** 2–3 sets of 10–15 reps, 2–3 days/week.
- **Progression:** Wall → knees → full plank; add a slow tempo. | **Regression:** Wall version with feet closer to the wall.
- **Cautions:** Keep within a pain-free arc; stop if the wrist or front of the shoulder complains, and shorten the lever (move to an easier variant).
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` (wall version) — with the phone propped at the wall facing the user, elbow flexion/extension is clearly visible (primary signal: elbow angle shoulder–elbow–wrist cycling); the subtle scapular "plus" itself is hard to verify. Floor versions: not-trackable from a front camera.
- **Sources:** [Physiopedia](https://www.physio-pedia.com/Push_Up_Plus_Exercise) (T2); [Peak Physio](https://www.peak-physio.com.au/exercise/serratus-anterior-elbow-wall-push-up/) (T3); [PMC push-up-plus EMG study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4327800/) (T1 literature)

### Shoulder Shrug
- **Aliases:** trap shrug, dumbbell shrug, scapular elevation
- **Position:** standing | seated
- **Equipment:** none | light dumbbell
- **Target:** upper trapezius, levator scapulae
- **Purpose:** Builds endurance in the upper trapezius, which — despite its reputation for tightness — is often weak and deconditioned in people with neck/shoulder tension. Gentle loaded shrugs can reduce, not increase, tension symptoms.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand tall, arms hanging at your sides (optionally holding light weights, palms facing in).
  2. Lift both shoulders straight up toward your ears as high as comfortable.
  3. Pause 1–2 seconds at the top.
  4. Lower the shoulders slowly all the way down.
  5. Repeat with smooth rhythm.
- **Form cues:** Elbows stay straight — arms are just hooks; straight up-and-down motion, no rolling under load; slow controlled lowering.
- **Common mistakes:** Jerky, bouncing reps; rolling the shoulders while holding weights; jutting the chin forward.
- **Typical dosage:** 3–4 sets of 15–20 reps (endurance focus), 2–3 days/week.
- **Progression:** Add or increase light weights; add a top hold. | **Regression:** No weight; smaller range.
- **Cautions:** Keep weights light initially; stop if it triggers headache or arm tingling.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — vertical oscillation of both shoulder keypoints toward the ears is a clean waist-up signal (primary signal: shoulder–ear vertical distance cycling); amplitude is modest, so calibrate per user.
- **Sources:** [Medbridge](https://www.medbridge.com/blog/best-exercises-for-the-trapezius-muscle) (T2); [POGO Physio](https://www.pogophysio.com.au/blog/upper-trap-shrug/) (T3); [Hinge Health](https://www.hingehealth.com/resources/articles/trapezius-pain-exercises/) (T3)

### Elbow Flexion (Biceps Curl)
- **Aliases:** biceps curl, arm curl
- **Position:** standing | seated
- **Equipment:** light dumbbell (or household item / resistance band)
- **Target:** biceps brachii (crosses the front of the shoulder), forearm flexors
- **Purpose:** Included in shoulder conditioning programs because the biceps tendon crosses and supports the front of the shoulder; strong elbow flexors share load during lifting and carrying.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand tall holding a light weight, arm at your side, palm forward.
  2. Keep the upper arm still against your side.
  3. Bend the elbow, curling the weight toward the shoulder.
  4. Pause briefly at the top.
  5. Lower slowly until the arm is straight.
  6. Repeat, then switch arms.
- **Form cues:** Upper arm pinned — only the forearm moves; full slow lowering each rep; no trunk lean or swing.
- **Common mistakes:** Swinging the weight with the hips; half-range reps; letting the shoulder roll forward at the top.
- **Typical dosage:** 3 sets of 8 building to 3 × 12, ~3 days/week; add weight gradually (AAOS caps around 2–5 kg for rehab purposes).
- **Progression:** Gradual weight increases; slower eccentric. | **Regression:** Band instead of weight; supported seated curl.
- **Cautions:** Keep loads modest during shoulder rehab; stop if the front of the shoulder aches during curls.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — classic repetitive joint-angle motion: elbow flexion angle (shoulder–elbow–wrist) cycling ~170°→~40°, fully visible waist-up from the front.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/rotator-cuff-and-shoulder-conditioning-program/) (T1); [NHS Live Well strength exercises](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/strength-exercises/) (T1)

### Elbow Extension (Overhead Triceps Extension)
- **Aliases:** triceps extension, overhead triceps press
- **Position:** standing | seated
- **Equipment:** light dumbbell
- **Target:** triceps brachii (long head crosses the shoulder), posterior arm
- **Purpose:** Part of comprehensive shoulder conditioning: the triceps' long head attaches at the shoulder blade, so strengthening it supports the shoulder during pushing and overhead tasks.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand or sit tall holding a light weight in the affected hand.
  2. Raise that arm overhead, then support the upper arm with the opposite hand.
  3. Bend the elbow, lowering the weight behind your head.
  4. Straighten the elbow, pressing the weight toward the ceiling.
  5. Lower slowly and repeat.
- **Form cues:** Keep the upper arm vertical and still; ribs down, no back arch; slow control behind the head.
- **Common mistakes:** Flaring the elbow wide; arching the low back; choosing too heavy a weight.
- **Typical dosage:** 3 sets of 8 building to 3 × 12, ~3 days/week, light weight.
- **Progression:** Small weight increments. | **Regression:** Band pressdowns with the arm at the side (no overhead position needed).
- **Cautions:** Requires comfortable overhead range — postpone until mobility allows; stop with any shoulder pinching.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — with the arm overhead, the elbow angle (shoulder–elbow–wrist) opens and closes visibly above the head from a front camera; upper-body framing is sufficient.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/rotator-cuff-and-shoulder-conditioning-program/) (T1); [StrengthLog](https://www.strengthlog.com/) (T3)

## Posture

### Chin Tuck
- **Aliases:** cervical retraction, neck retraction, double-chin exercise
- **Position:** seated | standing | supine
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** deep neck flexors (strengthened), suboccipitals and upper traps (lengthened)
- **Purpose:** The go-to corrective for forward-head posture, which loads the neck and shoulder girdle and feeds posture-related shoulder/neck tension. Re-teaches a head-over-shoulders position.
- **Steps:**
  1. Sit or stand tall, gaze level.
  2. Without tilting the head up or down, glide the chin straight back — as if making a gentle double chin.
  3. Feel the back of the neck lengthen; the movement is small (2–5 cm).
  4. Hold 5–10 seconds while breathing normally.
  5. Release back to neutral and repeat.
  6. Supine variant: perform lying down and lift the head ~5 cm while tucked, hold 5 seconds.
- **Form cues:** Glide back, don't nod down; keep jaw and shoulders relaxed; think "make yourself tall through the crown."
- **Common mistakes:** Nodding the chin to the chest instead of gliding back; jamming into an uncomfortable end range; shrugging during the hold.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 second holds × 10 reps, 2× daily.
- **Progression:** Supine head-lift variant; add gentle fingertip resistance on the chin. | **Regression:** Smaller glide; do it against a wall for feedback (head lightly touching).
- **Cautions:** Keep the movement small and painless; stop if it causes dizziness or radiating symptoms.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — the retraction is a small motion along the camera's depth axis; a front camera cannot measure the chin glide reliably (a side view could).
- **Sources:** [NASM](https://blog.nasm.org/correcting-upper-crossed-syndrome) (T2); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/posture-exercises) (T1); [Iron Neck guide](https://www.iron-neck.com/blogs/articles/chin-tuck-exercise) (T3)

### Scapular Retraction (Shoulder Blade Squeeze)
- **Aliases:** shoulder blade squeeze, scapular setting, shoulder blade pinch
- **Position:** seated | standing
- **Equipment:** none | chair
- **Target:** middle trapezius, rhomboids; lower trapezius (with the "down" component)
- **Purpose:** Wakes up the muscles that hold the shoulder blades back and down — the foundation position for a healthy shoulder. Frequently prescribed for rounded shoulders, desk-related tension, and as the starting cue for most shoulder strengthening.
- **Steps:**
  1. Sit on an armless chair or stand tall, arms relaxed at your sides.
  2. Draw both shoulder blades back toward each other, as if pinching a pencil between them.
  3. Then draw them gently downward, away from the ears.
  4. Hold 5 seconds while breathing normally.
  5. Relax fully.
  6. Repeat.
- **Form cues:** Squeeze back and down — not up; keep the chest open but ribs quiet (no back arch); shoulders stay away from ears.
- **Common mistakes:** Shrugging while squeezing; arching the low back; holding the breath.
- **Typical dosage:** 5-second holds × 10 reps, 2× daily (Mayo suggests 3–5 reps twice a day as a minimum).
- **Progression:** Add a resistance band (pull-apart) or perform against a table edge for resistance. | **Regression:** Shorter holds; lie on your back and press shoulder blades into the floor.
- **Cautions:** Gentle effort suffices; stop if it causes cramping between the blades — reduce hold time.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — the scapulae move behind the torso; from a front camera the residual signal (shoulders drifting slightly back/down) is too subtle and depth-based to count reps reliably.
- **Sources:** [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/img-20076263) (T1); [Versus Arthritis](https://versusarthritis.org/about-arthritis/exercising-with-arthritis/exercises-for-healthy-joints/exercises-for-the-shoulders/) (T1); [NASM](https://blog.nasm.org/correcting-upper-crossed-syndrome) (T2)

### Wall Angels
- **Aliases:** wall slides, wall W-to-Y slides, standing wall angels
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** wall
- **Target:** lower/middle trapezius, rhomboids, rear deltoids; stretches pecs and lats
- **Purpose:** Trains shoulder-blade control through an overhead arc while the wall enforces alignment — simultaneously stretching the tight front (chest) and strengthening the weak back. A flagship posture exercise for desk workers.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand with your back against a wall, feet a few centimeters out; press head, upper back, and tailbone lightly into the wall.
  2. Bring arms to a "goalpost/W" position: elbows bent 90°, backs of arms and hands touching the wall as much as possible.
  3. Slowly slide the arms up the wall toward a "Y"/overhead position, keeping contact.
  4. Go only as high as you can without losing wall contact or arching the back.
  5. Slide back down to the W, squeezing the shoulder blades down and together.
  6. Repeat with slow control.
- **Form cues:** Ribs down, low back close to the wall; keep wrists and elbows brushing the wall; move slowly — quality over height.
- **Common mistakes:** Arching the low back as arms rise; elbows and wrists peeling off the wall; shrugging into the ears.
- **Typical dosage:** 2–3 sets of 8–12 slow reps, daily or near-daily.
- **Progression:** Hold light weights; pause at the top; add a small band around the wrists. | **Regression:** Smaller arc; allow hands (not elbows) to leave the wall; do it seated on the floor against the wall.
- **Cautions:** Reduce the range if the shoulders pinch near the top; people with very tight chests should start with a partial arc.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — user faces the camera with back to the wall; both wrists rise and fall symmetrically overhead (primary signal: wrist height relative to shoulders/head plus elbow angle opening from ~90° toward extension each rep). Upper-body framing is sufficient.
- **Sources:** [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/posture-exercises) (T1); [NASM](https://blog.nasm.org/correcting-upper-crossed-syndrome) (T2); [Hinge Health](https://www.hingehealth.com/resources/articles/how-to-do-wall-angels/) (T3)

### Band Pull-Apart
- **Aliases:** resistance band pull-apart, horizontal band abduction
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** resistance band
- **Target:** middle trapezius, rhomboids, posterior deltoid, external rotators
- **Purpose:** Quick, portable strengthening for the upper-back muscles that counter rounded shoulders — a favorite antidote to long sitting and screen posture.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand tall holding a band in both hands, shoulder-width grip, arms extended forward at chest/shoulder height.
  2. Keep light starting tension on the band.
  3. Pull the hands apart, stretching the band toward your chest as the arms open wide.
  4. Squeeze the shoulder blades together and down as the band touches (or nears) the chest.
  5. Pause 1–2 seconds.
  6. Return slowly to the start with control.
- **Form cues:** Shoulders stay down while the blades squeeze; elbows nearly straight throughout; control the band on the way back — don't let it snap.
- **Common mistakes:** Shrugging as the arms open; bending the elbows and turning it into a row; arching the low back.
- **Typical dosage:** 2–3 sets of 10–15 reps, most days.
- **Progression:** Thicker band; pause reps; overhead pull-apart variant. | **Regression:** Lighter band; smaller range.
- **Cautions:** Choose a light band first — this movement fatigues quickly; stop if pinching occurs in the back of the shoulder.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — excellent front-camera exercise: both wrists move apart horizontally at shoulder height (primary signal: wrist-to-wrist separation distance cycling, with shoulder horizontal-abduction angle shoulder–elbow tracking as backup).
- **Sources:** [NASM](https://blog.nasm.org/correcting-upper-crossed-syndrome) (T2); [Back Intelligence](https://backintelligence.com/upper-crossed-syndrome-exercises/) (T3); [The PTDC](https://www.theptdc.com/articles/upper-crossed-syndrome) (T3)

### Face Pull
- **Aliases:** band face pull, high row to face, rear-delt pull
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** resistance band (anchored high)
- **Target:** posterior deltoid, middle/lower trapezius, external rotators
- **Purpose:** Combines a pull-back with outward rotation, hitting nearly every muscle that fights rounded-shoulder posture in one motion. Frequently programmed to balance pressing-heavy routines.
- **Steps:**
  1. Anchor a band at face height (or slightly above) on a door.
  2. Hold an end in each hand, arms extended toward the anchor, palms down or facing each other.
  3. Pull the band toward your face, elbows traveling high and wide.
  4. As the hands reach your ears, rotate the forearms so knuckles point toward the ceiling.
  5. Squeeze the shoulder blades together for 1–2 seconds.
  6. Return slowly and repeat.
- **Form cues:** Elbows high and wide — think "show your biceps" at the end; keep the shoulders down away from the ears; stand tall without leaning back.
- **Common mistakes:** Pulling low toward the chest (becomes a row); shrugging; using a band too heavy to control the end rotation.
- **Typical dosage:** 2–3 sets of 10–15 reps, 2–3 days/week.
- **Progression:** Thicker band, pause reps, half-kneeling position. | **Regression:** Lighter band, shorter range without the end rotation.
- **Cautions:** Postpone if the 90°-elevated position is painful; keep loads light until the movement is smooth.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — the pull travels along the camera's depth axis toward the face and the user faces the anchor point; elbow flare gives only a weak, ambiguous front-view signal.
- **Sources:** [NASM](https://blog.nasm.org/correcting-upper-crossed-syndrome) (T2); [The PTDC](https://www.theptdc.com/articles/upper-crossed-syndrome) (T3)

### Thoracic Extension over Foam Roller
- **Aliases:** foam roller upper-back extension, thoracic mobilization, upper-back opener
- **Position:** supine (over roller)
- **Equipment:** none required in variant form (foam roller preferred; rolled towel or chair-back as alternatives)
- **Target:** thoracic spine mobility; stretches pecs, mobilizes upper-back joints
- **Purpose:** A stiff, flexed upper back forces the shoulders to work in a bad position; extending over a roller restores the upper-back motion that healthy overhead reach and upright posture depend on.
- **Steps:**
  1. Sit on the floor, knees bent, with a foam roller behind you, horizontal, at mid-upper-back level (below the shoulder blades).
  2. Lie back over the roller, supporting your head with interlaced hands.
  3. Keep the ribs and core lightly braced so the low back stays neutral.
  4. Slowly arch the upper back over the roller, letting the chest open toward the ceiling.
  5. Hold a few seconds, breathing deeply, then return.
  6. Shift the roller up or down a segment and repeat along the mid-back for 1–2 minutes total.
- **Form cues:** Extend the upper back only — don't hinge at the low back; support the head to keep the neck relaxed; exhale into each extension.
- **Common mistakes:** Arching through the lumbar spine; placing the roller on the neck or low back; holding the breath.
- **Typical dosage:** 1–2 minutes of segments (or 8–10 slow extensions), daily.
- **Progression:** Arms reaching overhead during extension. | **Regression:** Use a rolled towel or drape over a chair back; smaller arch.
- **Cautions:** Avoid rolling the neck or lumbar spine; skip if you have osteoporosis or spine conditions without professional guidance; mild stretch only.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — supine over a roller on the floor; the extension arc is in the sagittal plane away from a front camera and the pose is heavily foreshortened.
- **Sources:** [NASM](https://blog.nasm.org/correcting-upper-crossed-syndrome) (T2); [Bob & Brad](https://www.bobandbrad.com/program/fix-shoulder-pain-permanently.-3-critical-exercises) (T3, tennis-ball variant); [Rehab Hero](https://www.rehabhero.ca/exercise/foam-roller-thoracic-extension) (T3)

### Levator Scapulae Stretch
- **Aliases:** armpit-sniff stretch, nose-to-armpit stretch
- **Position:** seated
- **Equipment:** chair
- **Target:** levator scapulae (neck-to-shoulder-blade muscle)
- **Purpose:** Releases the muscle that hikes the shoulder blade and stiffens with stress and desk work; complements the upper trap stretch for posture-related neck/shoulder tension.
- **Steps:**
  1. Sit tall and grip the chair seat with the hand on the side to be stretched.
  2. Turn your head ~45° away from that side.
  3. Drop your chin down, as if looking into your opposite armpit.
  4. Optionally add light overpressure with the free hand on the back of the head.
  5. Hold, then slowly return and switch sides.
- **Form cues:** Keep the anchored shoulder down; rotate first, then tuck; gentle pressure only.
- **Common mistakes:** Pulling hard on the head; letting the anchored shoulder hike; tilting instead of rotating-then-tucking.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 20–30 seconds, 2–3 reps per side, daily.
- **Progression:** Slightly longer holds. | **Regression:** No overpressure.
- **Cautions:** No nerve-type symptoms down the arm; ease off with dizziness.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-hold` — combined head rotation + flexion is visible from a front camera (signals: nose keypoint offset laterally and downward vs. shoulder line), though the rotation component reduces landmark confidence.
- **Note:** Multiple credible sources found (Spine-health, The Healthy, clinic sites) but no confirmed Tier 1/2 source during this extraction.
- **Sources:** [Spine-health](https://www.spine-health.com/wellness/exercise/easy-levator-scapulae-stretch-neck-pain) (T3); [Prevail Physio](https://www.prevailphysio.com/post/3-easy-neck-stretches) (T3)

### Tennis-Ball Thoracic Release + "W" Exercise (Bob & Brad)
- **Aliases:** posture reset over ball, standing W squeeze
- **Position:** supine (ball) then standing
- **Equipment:** none (tennis balls, wall optional)
- **Target:** thoracic mobility; middle/lower trapezius (W squeeze)
- **Purpose:** Bob & Brad's posture-first sequence for shoulder pain: mobilize the upper back over taped tennis balls, then actively hold a "W" arm position squeezing the shoulder blades.
- **Steps:**
  1. Tape two tennis balls together; lie back so they sit either side of the spine at the stiff upper-back level.
  2. Relax over them 30–60 seconds, moving gently.
  3. Stand and form a "W" with the arms (elbows bent, tucked toward the ribs).
  4. Squeeze shoulder blades back and down; hold several seconds; repeat ~10 times.
- **Form cues:** Balls beside the spine, never on it; keep ribs down in the W; shoulders away from ears.
- **Common mistakes:** Placing the balls on the neck/low back; arching the low back during the W.
- **Typical dosage:** Daily; W squeeze ~10 reps with 5-second holds.
- **Progression:** Perform the W against a wall or with a light band. | **Regression:** Foam roller instead of balls.
- **Cautions:** Avoid with spine fragility concerns; mild pressure only.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` for the standing W portion (elbow angle and wrist position forming the W, scapular squeeze inferred weakly); ball release portion not-trackable.
- **Sources:** [Bob & Brad](https://www.bobandbrad.com/program/fix-shoulder-pain-permanently.-3-critical-exercises) (T3)

### Pole-Assisted Arm Walk-Up
- **Aliases:** broomstick climb, Booyah Stik slide
- **Position:** seated | standing
- **Equipment:** none (broomstick or pole)
- **Target:** shoulder flexion range, assisted elevation
- **Purpose:** Bob & Brad's alternative to wall walking: the hand climbs a vertical pole anchored on the floor/chair, letting the pole take arm weight while range gradually increases.
- **Steps:**
  1. Anchor a broomstick vertically on the floor or against a chair.
  2. Grip low with the painful-side hand.
  3. Slide or walk the hand up the pole as far as comfort allows.
  4. Pause, then walk it back down.
- **Form cues:** Keep the shoulder relaxed; the pole carries the weight; no shrugging.
- **Common mistakes:** Forcing height; leaning the whole body to climb higher.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 climbs, 1–2× daily.
- **Progression:** Higher reach, less pole support. | **Regression:** Shorter climbs, seated.
- **Cautions:** Stay below the pain threshold; stop with pinching.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — wrist height climbing/descending along a visible vertical pole; primary signal: shoulder flexion angle (hip–shoulder–wrist).
- **Sources:** [Bob & Brad](https://www.bobandbrad.com/post/bob-brad-s-shoulder-pain-stretching-program) (T3)

### Handgrip Strengthening
- **Aliases:** grip squeezes, ball squeeze
- **Position:** seated | standing
- **Equipment:** none (soft ball or grip trainer)
- **Target:** forearm/grip muscles; reflex rotator cuff activation
- **Purpose:** A research trial found adding handgrip strengthening improved shoulder pain, cuff strength, and pain-free range in subacromial (impingement-type) shoulder pain — a low-irritability entry exercise.
- **Steps:**
  1. Hold a soft ball or grip trainer.
  2. Squeeze firmly but comfortably.
  3. Hold ~5 seconds, release fully.
  4. Repeat for the set on each hand.
- **Form cues:** Neutral wrist; shoulder relaxed while squeezing; full release between reps.
- **Common mistakes:** Shrugging while gripping; breath holding.
- **Typical dosage:** ~10 squeezes × 5-second holds, 2–3 sets daily.
- **Progression:** Firmer ball/resistance. | **Regression:** Softer ball, shorter holds.
- **Cautions:** Skip during acute hand/wrist issues.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — finger flexion force on a small object is below pose-landmark resolution.
- **Sources:** [PMC randomized trial](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9448609/) (T1 literature, single source)

### Shoulder Wheel / Arm Circles Progression
- **Aliases:** standing arm circles (large amplitude)
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** global shoulder mobility, circulation
- **Purpose:** Large controlled arm circles (clinic "shoulder wheel" analog) to groove smooth full-range motion once basic mobility returns.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand tall, arm relaxed at your side.
  2. Sweep the arm forward, up, back, and down in a large slow circle.
  3. Complete the set, then reverse direction.
- **Form cues:** Slow, even tempo; no shrug at the top; keep the trunk still.
- **Common mistakes:** Fast momentum-driven circles; arching the back at the top.
- **Typical dosage:** 10–15 circles each direction, daily.
- **Progression:** Larger circles, light weight. | **Regression:** Half circles below shoulder height.
- **Cautions:** Only after pain-free basic range is available; shrink the circle if any arc pinches.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — large cyclic wrist trajectory around the shoulder is highly visible from the front; primary signal: shoulder elevation angle (hip–shoulder–wrist) cycling through the circle.
- **Sources:** [Wright PT](https://wrightpt.com/physical-therapy-for-frozen-shoulder/) (T3, single source; NHS Ayrshire's swing variants were merged into Pendulum Swing above)
