# Knee — Exercise Knowledge Base
Extraction date: 2026-08-03. Sourcing rules: see knowledge-base/README.md.
Corroborated exercises: 26. Candidates: 3.

Scope: knee pain relief, patellofemoral (kneecap) pain, knee osteoarthritis, and general knee strengthening/mobility. General guidance repeated across Tier 1 sources: warm up 5–10 minutes with light activity before the session; keep pain at or below roughly 3–5/10 during exercise; mild next-day muscle soreness is acceptable but symptoms should not be worse overall; strength work 2–4 days per week, stretches most days.

## Stretches & Mobility

### Heel Slide
- **Aliases:** knee-flexion slide, sliding knee bend, seated knee bend
- **Position:** supine (or long-sitting on a bed/floor; can be adapted to seated in a chair)
- **Equipment:** none (optional towel or plastic bag under the heel to reduce friction)
- **Target:** knee joint range of motion (flexion/extension); gentle activation of hamstrings and quadriceps
- **Purpose:** Restores comfortable bending and straightening of a stiff or achy knee and "oils" the joint before other exercise. Frequently used as a warm-up and as a first-line mobility drill for arthritic or stiff knees.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back (or sit with legs out straight) with both legs extended.
  2. Slowly slide the heel of the affected leg along the surface toward your buttock, letting the knee bend as far as is comfortable.
  3. Pause for about 2 seconds at your comfortable end point.
  4. Slide the heel back out until the leg is fully straight again.
  5. That is one repetition; continue at a slow, even pace.
- **Form cues:** Keep the heel in contact with the surface the whole time; move slowly and let comfort set the depth; keep the kneecap and toes pointing up so the leg tracks straight.
- **Common mistakes:** Yanking the leg into a painful range instead of easing into it; letting the knee fall inward or outward as it bends; holding the breath and tensing through the movement.
- **Typical dosage:** 10 repetitions per leg, 1–3 sessions per day; hold the bent position 5+ seconds as flexibility improves.
- **Progression:** Use a strap around the ankle to gently add a little more bend at the end range, or progress to seated heel slides under a chair. | **Regression:** Reduce the depth of the bend, or do the movement on a smooth surface with a plastic bag under the heel.
- **Cautions:** Ease off if the knee is hot, swollen, or sharply painful; keep movement within a mild-discomfort range.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` in the seated (chair or long-sit facing the camera) variant — knee flexion angle: hip–knee–ankle; the supine floor version is poorly visible to a front phone camera.
- **Sources:** [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/knee-arthritis-exercises) (T1); [NHS Inform](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/muscle-bone-and-joints/leg-and-foot-problems-and-conditions/exercises-for-osteoarthritis-of-the-knee/) (T1); [Ask Doctor Jo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/real-time-knee-stretches/) (T3)

### Leg Swing (Pendulum)
- **Aliases:** pendulum knee swing, relaxed leg swing
- **Position:** standing (or seated on a high surface with the leg hanging free)
- **Equipment:** chair or wall for support
- **Target:** knee joint mobility; relaxed movement of quadriceps and hamstrings
- **Purpose:** Gently moves the knee through range without load, easing stiffness and helping a sore or arthritic knee loosen up. Studied as a low-strain mobility exercise for knee osteoarthritis.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand tall next to a chair, counter, or wall and hold on for balance.
  2. Shift your weight onto the unaffected leg.
  3. Let the affected leg hang relaxed and swing it gently forward and backward like a pendulum.
  4. Start with small swings and gradually let the arc grow as the knee loosens.
  5. Continue for the prescribed time or repetitions, keeping the leg loose throughout.
- **Form cues:** Keep the swinging leg relaxed — momentum, not muscle effort, drives the motion; stay tall through the trunk; keep the swing smooth and rhythmic.
- **Common mistakes:** Forcing the swing higher with muscle effort; leaning heavily onto the support and losing upright posture; swinging fast enough to provoke pain.
- **Typical dosage:** 20–30 relaxed swings per leg, 1–3 times daily; increase amplitude gradually.
- **Progression:** Larger arcs, or perform seated on a high surface letting gravity bend the knee further. | **Regression:** Smaller swings with more hand support.
- **Cautions:** Stop if swinging provokes catching, giving way, or sharp pain.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — knee flexion angle (hip–knee–ankle) and ankle displacement oscillate rhythmically and are visible from a front view.
- **Sources:** [NHS Inform](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/muscle-bone-and-joints/leg-and-foot-problems-and-conditions/exercises-for-osteoarthritis-of-the-knee/) (T1); [peer-reviewed trial protocol, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9014577/) (T1)

### Standing Quadriceps Stretch
- **Aliases:** quad stretch, heel-to-buttock stretch
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** wall or chair for balance (optional towel/strap around the ankle)
- **Target:** quadriceps and front of the hip
- **Purpose:** Tight quadriceps increase pressure on the kneecap; lengthening them reduces anterior knee strain and improves comfort in walking and stairs.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand tall beside a wall or chair and rest one hand on it for balance.
  2. Bend the knee of the leg to be stretched and grasp the ankle (or a strap looped around it) behind you.
  3. Gently draw the heel toward your buttock until you feel a stretch along the front of the thigh.
  4. Keep the knees close together and the thigh pointing straight down.
  5. Hold, then slowly release and repeat on the other side.
- **Form cues:** Tighten the abdominals so the lower back doesn't arch; keep the bent knee pointing down, not out to the side; stretch to tension, never to pain.
- **Common mistakes:** Letting the knee drift forward or out sideways; arching the back to fake a bigger stretch; bouncing.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 20–30 seconds (up to 45–60 seconds per AAOS), 2–4 repetitions per leg, daily or after activity.
- **Progression:** Increase hold time; perform lying on your side or prone with a strap for a deeper stretch. | **Regression:** Rest the foot on a low chair behind you, or do the stretch lying on your side, if grasping the ankle is difficult.
- **Cautions:** Skip or shorten range if deep knee bending is painful or if balance is unreliable without solid support.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-hold` — a static pose with high knee flexion (ankle near hip) and upright trunk is checkable from a front view via the hip–knee–ankle angle and ankle-to-hip distance.
- **Sources:** [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/fitness/in-depth/stretching/art-20546848) (T1); [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.knee-arthritis-exercises.bo1534) (T1)

### Supine Hamstring Stretch (with Strap)
- **Aliases:** lying hamstring stretch, towel hamstring stretch, doorway hamstring stretch
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** towel, strap, or belt (doorway/wall variant needs no strap)
- **Target:** hamstrings (back of the thigh)
- **Purpose:** Tight hamstrings keep the knee slightly bent and increase joint loading; stretching them helps the knee straighten fully and reduces pain behind the knee and with walking.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back with both legs out straight (one knee may be bent with foot flat for comfort).
  2. Loop a towel or strap around the sole of one foot.
  3. Keeping the leg straight or nearly straight, use the strap to raise the leg toward the ceiling.
  4. Stop when you feel a comfortable stretch along the back of the thigh.
  5. Hold, then slowly lower the leg and repeat on the other side.
- **Form cues:** Keep the pelvis and lower back settled on the floor; pull to gentle tension only; keep the neck and shoulders relaxed.
- **Common mistakes:** Bending the knee so much that the stretch disappears; hauling the leg up fast; lifting the hips off the floor.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 20–30 seconds (Kaiser: 15–30 s), 2–4 repetitions per leg, daily.
- **Progression:** Move the leg further toward vertical, or use the doorway version and scoot closer to the frame over time. | **Regression:** Bend the resting leg, keep a small bend in the stretching knee, or rest the raised heel against a wall/door frame and let gravity do the work.
- **Cautions:** Avoid pulling into nerve-type symptoms (tingling down the leg); keep the stretch gentle.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — floor-lying with the motion mostly in a plane the front camera can't judge reliably; landmark visibility is poor when supine.
- **Sources:** [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.knee-arthritis-exercises.bo1534) (T1); [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/hamstring-injury/multimedia/hamstring-stretch/img-20006930) (T1); [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) (T1); [WebMD](https://www.webmd.com/osteoarthritis/ss/slideshow-knee-exercises) (T3)

### Standing Calf Stretch
- **Aliases:** heel-cord stretch, gastrocnemius/wall calf stretch
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** wall (or sturdy chair)
- **Target:** calf muscles (gastrocnemius/soleus) and Achilles region
- **Purpose:** Tight calves alter ankle motion and shift extra load to the knee; stretching them supports better squatting, stair, and walking mechanics for sore knees.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand facing a wall and place both hands on it at about chest height.
  2. Step one leg back, keeping that back leg straight and its heel flat on the floor.
  3. Bend the front knee and shift your hips toward the wall.
  4. Feel the stretch in the calf of the back leg.
  5. Hold, then switch legs.
- **Form cues:** Keep the back heel glued to the floor; toes of both feet pointing at the wall; keep the back leg straight for the upper calf, or bend it slightly to bias the lower calf.
- **Common mistakes:** Letting the back heel lift; turning the back foot outward; bouncing into the stretch.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 15–30 seconds, 2–4 repetitions per leg, daily.
- **Progression:** Step the back foot further away, or drop the heel off a step edge. | **Regression:** Smaller stance, less lean.
- **Cautions:** Keep the stretch pain-free at the Achilles; use stable support if balance is limited.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — the user faces the wall (side-on or away from a propped phone) and the staggered-stance depth is ambiguous from a front view.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.knee-arthritis-exercises.bo1534) (T1); [WebMD](https://www.webmd.com/osteoarthritis/ss/slideshow-knee-exercises) (T3)

### Standing IT Band Stretch
- **Aliases:** iliotibial band stretch, cross-behind side lean
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** wall for balance (optional)
- **Target:** iliotibial band and outer hip/thigh (tensor fasciae latae, gluteal fascia)
- **Purpose:** A tight IT band contributes to outer-knee and kneecap pain; this stretch eases lateral tension on the knee, and is included in NHS knee routines for runners.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand tall, near a wall if you want support.
  2. Cross the affected leg behind the other leg.
  3. Reach the arm on the affected side overhead and lean your upper body away from the affected side.
  4. Push the hip of the affected side gently outward until you feel a stretch along the outer hip/thigh.
  5. Hold, return upright, and repeat; then switch sides.
- **Form cues:** Lean sideways, not forward; keep both feet grounded; feel it along the outer hip or thigh — some feel it nearer the knee.
- **Common mistakes:** Twisting the trunk instead of side-bending; crossing the legs too tightly and losing balance; rushing the hold.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold about 30 seconds, 3–4 repetitions per side, daily or after activity.
- **Progression:** Increase the lean and hip push, add the overhead reach. | **Regression:** Hold a wall and use a smaller lean.
- **Cautions:** Ease off if you feel pinching at the hip or any loss of balance; the sensation should be a stretch, not pain.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-hold` — static pose with detectable ankle crossing and lateral trunk lean (shoulder–hip line tilt) from a front view.
- **Sources:** [HSS](https://www.hss.edu/health-library/move-better/it-band-stretches) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/it-band-stretches) (T1); [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/knee-exercises-for-runners/) (T1); [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/exercise-and-fitness/preventing-and-treating-iliotibial-it-band-syndrome-tips-for-pain-free-movement) (T1)

### Kneeling Hip Flexor Stretch
- **Aliases:** half-kneeling lunge stretch, couch-adjacent hip flexor stretch
- **Position:** quadruped-adjacent (half-kneeling)
- **Equipment:** towel or cushion under the knee
- **Target:** hip flexors (iliopsoas, rectus femoris) of the rear leg
- **Purpose:** Tight hip flexors tilt the pelvis and change how load reaches the kneecap; stretching them is commonly paired with quad stretching to relieve front-of-knee pain.
- **Steps:**
  1. Kneel on the affected-side knee on a folded towel; place the other foot flat on the floor in front, knee bent about 90 degrees.
  2. Keep your trunk upright and tighten your buttock on the kneeling side.
  3. Shift your hips gently forward until you feel a stretch across the front of the kneeling-side hip and upper thigh.
  4. Hold the position, breathing normally.
  5. Return, rest, repeat, then switch sides.
- **Form cues:** Stay tall — don't arch the lower back; the movement is a forward hip shift, not a deep lunge; squeeze the glute of the rear leg to deepen the stretch.
- **Common mistakes:** Overarching the lower back instead of moving the hips; letting the front knee drift past the toes; kneeling on a hard surface.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 15–30 seconds (up to 45 s), 2–4 repetitions per side, daily.
- **Progression:** Reach the same-side arm overhead or add a slight side lean away. | **Regression:** Do a standing version with the rear leg extended and hands on a chair, if kneeling is uncomfortable.
- **Cautions:** Pad the kneecap well; skip the kneeling version during a flare of kneecap pain and use the standing variant instead.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-hold` — half-kneeling stance (one knee down, one hip/knee at ~90°) is a distinct static pose from a front view, though forward hip shift itself is subtle.
- **Sources:** [MyHealth Alberta](https://myhealth.alberta.ca/Health/pages/conditions.aspx?hwid=hw208017) (T1); [ACE Fitness](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/142/kneeling-hip-flexor-stretch/) (T2); [HSS](https://www.hss.edu/health-library/move-better/hip-flexor-stretch) (T1); [Ask Doctor Jo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/real-time-knee-stretches/) (T3)

### Low-Impact Aerobic Warm-Up (Stationary Cycling / Walking)
- **Aliases:** exercise-bike warm-up, low-resistance cycling, brisk walking, water aerobics (pool variant)
- **Position:** seated (bike) or standing (walking)
- **Equipment:** stationary bike, or none for walking
- **Target:** whole lower limb — quadriceps, hamstrings, calves, hips — plus cardiovascular fitness
- **Purpose:** Smooth, repetitive, low-load motion lubricates the knee, warms the muscles before stretching/strengthening, and is itself one of the best-evidenced exercise modes for easing knee osteoarthritis pain (large pooled analyses favor walking, cycling, and swimming).
- **Steps:**
  1. Set the bike seat so the knee stays slightly bent at the bottom of the pedal stroke (or choose a flat walking route).
  2. Begin at an easy, low-resistance pace.
  3. Continue 5–10 minutes as a warm-up before knee exercises.
  4. For a conditioning session, build gradually toward 20–30 minutes at a comfortable, conversational effort.
  5. Finish with gentle stretches while the muscles are warm.
- **Form cues:** Keep resistance light at first; pedal or walk with a smooth rhythm; on the bike, avoid a seat so low that the knee bends deeply under load.
- **Common mistakes:** Starting with heavy resistance or hills; seat too low (increases kneecap pressure); doing too much too soon after a sedentary period.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 minutes as warm-up before every exercise session; toward 150 minutes/week of moderate low-impact aerobic activity overall.
- **Progression:** Longer duration first, then modest resistance/pace increases. | **Regression:** Shorter bouts (even 5–10 minutes), partial pedal rocking if a full revolution is not yet comfortable, or pool-based walking/aerobics to unload the joint.
- **Cautions:** Choose flat terrain and avoid deep-knee-bend resistance work during painful flares; swap to water-based exercise if land walking aggravates symptoms.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — equipment-based or locomotor activity that moves out of a fixed phone frame.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/bones-and-joints/walking-cycling-and-swimming-are-best-exercises-for-knee-osteoarthritis) (T1); [Arthritis Foundation](https://www.arthritis.org/health-wellness/healthy-living/physical-activity/getting-started/benefits-of-exercise-for-osteoarthritis) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/knee-arthritis-exercises) (T1)

## Strengthening

### Quadriceps Set
- **Aliases:** quad set, static quad contraction, towel press
- **Position:** supine or long-sitting (leg straight)
- **Equipment:** none (optional small rolled towel under the knee)
- **Target:** quadriceps (especially vastus medialis), knee extension mechanism
- **Purpose:** Wakes up and strengthens the quadriceps without moving or loading the joint, so it is safe even for irritable, arthritic, or recently painful knees. Foundational first-step strengthening in nearly every knee program.
- **Steps:**
  1. Sit or lie with the affected leg straight; place a small rolled towel under the knee if you like a target.
  2. Pull the toes gently toward you.
  3. Tighten the muscle on the front of the thigh, pressing the back of the knee down into the floor/towel.
  4. Feel the kneecap draw slightly upward as the thigh firms.
  5. Hold the contraction, then fully relax.
  6. Repeat.
- **Form cues:** Push the knee down "as hard as comfortable" into the surface; keep breathing during the hold; watch the thigh muscle visibly tighten to confirm activation.
- **Common mistakes:** Holding the breath; lifting the heel or hip instead of pressing the knee down; contracting so briefly that the muscle never fully engages.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 3–10 seconds, 8–12 repetitions, 1–3 sessions daily (Kaiser: 6-second holds × 8–12; Ask Doctor Jo: 3–5 s × 10).
- **Progression:** Longer holds, then advance to short-arc quads and straight leg raises. | **Regression:** Shorter, gentler holds; contract only to the point of comfort.
- **Cautions:** Should be pain-free; skip during acute joint inflammation with resting pain.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — an isometric contraction with almost no visible joint-angle change; too subtle for pose landmarks.
- **Sources:** [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.knee-arthritis-exercises.bo1534) (T1); [Versus Arthritis](https://www.versusarthritis.org/about-arthritis/exercising-with-arthritis/exercises-for-healthy-joints/exercises-for-the-knees/) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/knee-arthritis-exercises) (T1); [Ask Doctor Jo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/knee-osteoarthritis/) (T3)

### Terminal Knee Extension (Short-Arc Quad)
- **Aliases:** short-arc quadriceps extension, SAQ, TKE, banded standing TKE (variant)
- **Position:** supine/long-sitting (towel-roll version) or standing (band version)
- **Equipment:** towel (rolled) or resistance band
- **Target:** quadriceps in the final degrees of knee straightening
- **Purpose:** Trains the quads exactly where they stabilize the kneecap and control the last part of straightening — a range that is often weak in knee pain and osteoarthritis — with very low joint stress.
- **Steps:**
  1. Place a firm rolled towel or bolster under the affected knee so it rests slightly bent.
  2. Tighten the front of the thigh and straighten the knee, lifting the heel off the surface.
  3. Keep the back of the knee in contact with the roll.
  4. Hold the straight position for 2–5 seconds.
  5. Slowly lower the heel back down and relax.
  6. (Band variant: stand with a band looped behind the knee and anchored ahead of you; slowly straighten the knee against the band's pull.)
- **Form cues:** The knee stays on the roll — only the heel lifts; squeeze the thigh hard at the top; lower with control rather than dropping.
- **Common mistakes:** Lifting the whole thigh off the roll (turning it into a leg raise); rushing the lowering phase; using a roll so large the arc becomes painful.
- **Typical dosage:** 8–15 repetitions with 2–5 second holds, 2–3 sets, most days.
- **Progression:** Add a light ankle weight, or move to the standing band TKE. | **Regression:** Smaller towel roll (smaller arc) or return to quad sets.
- **Cautions:** Keep the arc small and pain-free; avoid heavy ankle weights early on.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — floor-based with a small heel-lift arc that reads poorly from a front phone camera; the standing band variant is side-on and subtle.
- **Sources:** [NASM](https://blog.nasm.org/fitness/exercises-for-knee-pain) (T2); [Rehab Hero](https://www.rehabhero.ca/exercise/terminal-knee-extension) (T3); [Bob & Brad](https://www.bobandbrad.com/health-programs/knee-pain-relief-program) (T3)

### Straight Leg Raise (Supine)
- **Aliases:** SLR, single-leg lift, lying leg raise
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** none (optional ankle weight for progression)
- **Target:** quadriceps and hip flexors
- **Purpose:** Strengthens the quads with the knee locked straight so the joint itself barely moves — a staple for building knee support when bending is painful (arthritis, kneecap pain, post-injury).
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back, propped on elbows if comfortable.
  2. Bend the unaffected knee and place that foot flat on the floor.
  3. Straighten the affected leg fully and pull the toes toward you.
  4. Tighten the thigh, then lift the straight leg until it is level with the bent knee (roughly 30–45 cm off the floor).
  5. Hold briefly (up to 5 seconds), then lower slowly with control.
  6. Relax fully between repetitions.
- **Form cues:** Lock the knee straight before lifting and keep it locked; lead with a tight thigh, not a swinging hip; keep the lower back settled.
- **Common mistakes:** Letting the knee bend during the lift; lifting too high or too fast with momentum; arching the lower back.
- **Typical dosage:** 10–15 repetitions, 2–3 sets per leg, once daily (Cleveland: 10–15 reps; Arthritis Foundation: 2 × 10).
- **Progression:** Add 0.5–1 kg ankle weights in small steps, or point the toes outward slightly to bias the inner quad. | **Regression:** Smaller lift height, no hold, or start with quad sets.
- **Cautions:** Keep the movement smooth; stop if you feel back strain and re-set the pelvis.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — supine floor exercise; hip flexion of a straight leg is hard to measure reliably from a front phone view.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/exercises-for-patellofemoral-pain-syndrome-pfps) (T1); [Arthritis Foundation](https://www.arthritis.org/health-wellness/healthy-living/physical-activity/getting-started/your-exercise-solution/lower-body-movements/knee-movements) (T1); [Mayo Clinic](https://newsnetwork.mayoclinic.org/discussion/recommended-exercises-for-knee-pain-depend-on-the-cause-of-the-pain/) (T1); [Ask Doctor Jo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/knee-osteoarthritis/) (T3)

### Seated Knee Extension (Long-Arc Quad)
- **Aliases:** LAQ, seated leg extension, seated straight-leg raise (chair version)
- **Position:** seated
- **Equipment:** chair (optional ankle weight or light band for progression)
- **Target:** quadriceps
- **Purpose:** Builds the thigh strength that supports standing, walking, and stairs, using the full available arc of knee straightening; simple, chair-based, and beginner-friendly for arthritic knees.
- **Steps:**
  1. Sit tall in a chair with your back supported, feet flat, knees at about 90 degrees.
  2. Slowly straighten one knee, lifting the foot until the leg is as straight as comfortable.
  3. Pull the toes toward you and squeeze the thigh at the top.
  4. Hold for a slow count (Versus Arthritis suggests up to 10 seconds).
  5. Lower the foot slowly back to the floor.
  6. Repeat, then switch legs.
- **Form cues:** Keep the thigh resting on the seat — only the lower leg moves; move slowly both directions; sit tall rather than slumping.
- **Common mistakes:** Swinging the leg with momentum; leaning back to hoist the leg; dropping the foot instead of lowering it.
- **Typical dosage:** 10–15 repetitions per leg, 2–3 sets, daily to every other day; add holds of 5–10 seconds at the top.
- **Progression:** Ankle weight in small increments, longer holds, or a band anchored behind the chair leg. | **Regression:** Straighten only partway, no hold.
- **Cautions:** If the kneecap grumbles mid-range, limit the arc to the comfortable upper portion; keep loads light during flares.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — seated facing the camera, the knee flexion angle (hip–knee–ankle) sweeps ~90°→~0° each rep; clear, repetitive signal.
- **Sources:** [Versus Arthritis](https://www.versusarthritis.org/about-arthritis/exercising-with-arthritis/exercises-for-healthy-joints/exercises-for-the-knees/) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/knee-arthritis-exercises) (T1); [More Life Health](https://morelifehealth.com/seated-knee-extensions) (T3); [Ask Doctor Jo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VuJZ6dqMf8M) (T3)

### Prone Straight Leg Raise
- **Aliases:** prone leg lift, prone hip extension
- **Position:** prone
- **Equipment:** none (optional pillow under pelvis; ankle weight for progression)
- **Target:** gluteus maximus and hamstrings
- **Purpose:** Strengthens the hip extensors that share load with the knee during walking and stairs, without bending the knee — useful when the front of the knee is sensitive.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie face down with legs straight, head resting on your arms (pillow under the pelvis optional).
  2. Tighten the buttock and hamstring of one leg.
  3. Keeping the knee straight, lift that leg toward the ceiling as high as comfortable.
  4. Hold about 5 seconds.
  5. Lower slowly and rest 2 seconds.
  6. Repeat, then switch legs.
- **Form cues:** Keep both hip bones in contact with the floor; let the buttock, not the lower back, drive the lift; keep the neck relaxed.
- **Common mistakes:** Arching/twisting the lower back to lift higher; bending the knee; jerking the leg up.
- **Typical dosage:** 10 repetitions per leg, building to 3 sets, 3–5 days per week; 5-second holds.
- **Progression:** Add ankle weight gradually (≈0.5 kg steps). | **Regression:** Smaller lift, no hold.
- **Cautions:** Keep the lift low if you feel pinching in the lower back; place a pillow under the hips.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — prone floor position; landmarks are occluded and the lift is directed away from a front camera.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Hinge Health](https://www.hingehealth.com/resources/articles/straight-leg-raise/) (T3); [WorkoutLabs](https://workoutlabs.com/exercise-guide/prone-lying-leg-lifts/) (T3)

### Hamstring Curl
- **Aliases:** standing knee curl, prone knee flexion, heel-to-buttock curl
- **Position:** standing (chair-supported) or prone
- **Equipment:** chair for support (optional ankle weight or band)
- **Target:** hamstrings
- **Purpose:** Balanced hamstring strength stabilizes the knee from behind and shares braking load with the quads — a core component of the AAOS knee program and most NHS knee sheets.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand tall holding the back of a chair (or lie face down with legs straight).
  2. Shift weight to the supporting leg.
  3. Bend the affected knee, bringing the heel up toward the buttock as far as comfortable.
  4. Hold briefly at the top.
  5. Lower the foot slowly back to the floor.
  6. Repeat, then switch legs.
- **Form cues:** Keep the thighs level and knees together — only the lower leg moves; move slowly both ways; stand tall without tipping forward.
- **Common mistakes:** Letting the working thigh swing forward; arching the back (especially prone); using momentum.
- **Typical dosage:** 10–15 repetitions per leg, 2–3 sets, 3–5 days per week; AAOS progresses toward ~4.5 kg (10 lb) ankle weight over weeks.
- **Progression:** Ankle weight or band resistance; slow the lowering phase. | **Regression:** Smaller arc, fingertip support on a wall.
- **Cautions:** Cramping hamstrings mean too much too soon — shorten the arc and build gradually.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — standing version facing the camera gives a clear repetitive knee flexion angle (hip–knee–ankle) as the heel rises toward the buttock.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) (T1); [NHS (UHS leaflet)](https://www.uhs.nhs.uk/Media/UHS-website-2019/Patientinformation/Medicinestherapiesandanaesthetics/Knee-strengthening-exercises-3923-PIL.pdf) (T1); [WebMD](https://www.webmd.com/osteoarthritis/ss/slideshow-knee-exercises) (T3); [Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/311138) (T3)

### Glute Bridge
- **Aliases:** bridging, hip raise, floor bridge
- **Position:** supine
- **Equipment:** none (optional mini-band above the knees)
- **Target:** gluteus maximus, hamstrings, core
- **Purpose:** Strong hips keep the thigh and knee aligned under load; bridging builds that support with zero impact, which helps both arthritic and kneecap-pain knees.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back with knees bent, feet flat and hip-width apart, arms by your sides.
  2. Tighten your abdominals and squeeze your buttocks.
  3. Lift your hips until the body forms a straight line from shoulders to knees.
  4. Pause about 2 seconds at the top.
  5. Lower slowly, ideally one segment of the spine at a time.
  6. Repeat.
- **Form cues:** Push through the heels; squeeze the glutes at the top rather than arching the back; keep knees tracking over the feet (don't let them fall in or out).
- **Common mistakes:** Overarching the lower back at the top; letting the knees splay or collapse inward; dropping down quickly.
- **Typical dosage:** 10 repetitions with a ~2-second top pause, 2–3 sets, 2–4 days per week.
- **Progression:** Mini-band above the knees, single-leg bridge, or feet elevated. | **Regression:** Smaller lift height; rest between each rep.
- **Cautions:** Keep the range comfortable for the lower back; avoid breath-holding.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — supine floor exercise; hip elevation is hard to quantify from a typical propped front camera.
- **Sources:** [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/knee-arthritis-exercises) (T1); [NASM](https://blog.nasm.org/exercise-programming-for-knee-replacements) (T2); [Ask Doctor Jo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/knee-osteoarthritis/) (T3)

### Clamshell
- **Aliases:** clam, side-lying knee lift
- **Position:** side-lying
- **Equipment:** none (optional mini-band above the knees)
- **Target:** gluteus medius and hip external rotators
- **Purpose:** Strengthens the outer-hip muscles that stop the knee collapsing inward during walking, stairs, and squatting — a key exercise for kneecap (patellofemoral) pain.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your side with hips and knees bent (~45–90 degrees), legs stacked, head supported.
  2. Keep the feet touching each other.
  3. Lift the top knee up toward the ceiling as far as you can without rolling the pelvis back.
  4. Hold up to 5 seconds.
  5. Lower the knee slowly to the start.
  6. Complete the set, then roll over and switch sides.
- **Form cues:** Pelvis stays stacked and still — imagine your back against a wall; feet stay glued together; the motion is small and controlled.
- **Common mistakes:** Rolling the hips backward to fake a bigger lift; lifting the feet apart; going fast.
- **Typical dosage:** 10–15 repetitions per side, up to 3 sets, 2–3 days per week (Cleveland: 15 reps with 5-second holds).
- **Progression:** Mini-band above the knees; add a brief top hold. | **Regression:** Smaller lift; lean the back against a wall for feedback.
- **Cautions:** Should feel work in the side of the hip, not pain in the knee or lower back.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — side-lying floor exercise; landmarks overlap and the lifting knee is difficult to angle-track from a front phone view.
- **Sources:** [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/exercises-for-patellofemoral-pain-syndrome-pfps) (T1); [InformedHealth / IQWiG](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK561509/) (T1); [ChoosePT](https://www.choosept.com/health-tips/Five-exercises-reduce-knee-pain) (T2)

### Side-Lying Hip Abduction
- **Aliases:** side leg raise, side-lying leg lift, straight-leg abduction
- **Position:** side-lying
- **Equipment:** none (optional ankle weight)
- **Target:** gluteus medius, outer thigh (abductors)
- **Purpose:** Hip abductor strength keeps the pelvis level and the knee tracking straight; weakness here is strongly linked to knee pain, so this lift is prescribed across arthritis and kneecap-pain programs.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your side with the bottom leg bent for stability and the top leg straight, body in one line.
  2. Tighten the thigh of the top leg and flex the foot.
  3. Raise the straight top leg toward the ceiling about 30–45 degrees.
  4. Hold briefly.
  5. Lower slowly without letting the leg rest fully between reps (or rest, if needed).
  6. Complete the set and switch sides.
- **Form cues:** Keep hips stacked and slightly forward — don't roll back; lead with the heel, toes pointing forward (not up); keep the trunk still.
- **Common mistakes:** Hiking the hip toward the ribs instead of lifting from the hip joint; rolling backward so the hip flexors take over; swinging fast.
- **Typical dosage:** 10–20 repetitions per side, 2–3 sets, 3–5 days per week (AAOS: 3 × 20 progressing to ankle weights).
- **Progression:** Ankle weight (build gradually toward ~4.5 kg per AAOS), or perform standing against band resistance. | **Regression:** Bend the top knee slightly or reduce lift height.
- **Cautions:** Work should be felt in the side of the hip; stop if it shifts to the lower back.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — side-lying floor position with stacked-limb occlusion from a front camera; the standing variant would be trackable instead.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/healthbeat/exercise-for-stronger-knees-and-hips) (T1); [ChoosePT](https://www.choosept.com/health-tips/Five-exercises-reduce-knee-pain) (T2)

### Isometric Leg Cross
- **Aliases:** ankle-over-ankle press, crossed-leg squeeze
- **Position:** seated (edge of chair, bed, or table)
- **Equipment:** chair
- **Target:** quadriceps (front leg) and hamstrings (back leg), co-contraction around the knee
- **Purpose:** Strengthens both sides of the knee at once with no joint movement — a discreet, equipment-free isometric used in Versus Arthritis and NHS runner routines.
- **Steps:**
  1. Sit on the edge of a seat or bed and cross your ankles, one in front of the other.
  2. Press the back leg forward and the front leg backward against each other.
  3. Build tension until the thigh muscles tighten firmly.
  4. Hold the squeeze as long as comfortable (aim ~10 seconds at first).
  5. Relax fully, rest, and repeat.
  6. Swap which leg is in front and repeat.
- **Form cues:** Push evenly with both legs so nothing actually moves; keep breathing through the hold; sit tall.
- **Common mistakes:** Holding the breath; pressing so hard it hurts; forgetting to swap leg positions.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold to comfortable fatigue (or ~10–20 seconds), rest 1 minute, 3 rounds per leg position; can be done with legs straight or bent.
- **Progression:** Longer or firmer holds; repeat at different knee angles. | **Regression:** Gentler pressure, shorter holds.
- **Cautions:** Effort should stay below pain; ease pressure if the kneecap aches.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — isometric with no visible movement; opposing forces cannot be inferred from pose landmarks.
- **Sources:** [Versus Arthritis](https://www.versusarthritis.org/about-arthritis/exercising-with-arthritis/exercises-for-healthy-joints/exercises-for-the-knees/) (T1); [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/knee-exercises-for-runners/) (T1)

### Wall Squat (Wall Sit / Wall Slide)
- **Aliases:** wall sit, wall slide, back-against-wall squat
- **Position:** standing (back against wall)
- **Equipment:** wall
- **Target:** quadriceps, glutes, hamstrings, core
- **Purpose:** Builds thigh and hip strength with the back supported and depth fully controllable, making squatting strength accessible to painful knees; a first-choice exercise for runner's knee and knee OA.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand with your back flat against a wall, feet hip-width apart and roughly 15–30 cm (6–12 in) from the wall, toes pointing slightly out.
  2. Slide your back down the wall by bending the knees.
  3. Stop at a partial depth — about a 45-degree knee bend (thighs well above parallel); Mayo suggests even ~30 degrees to start.
  4. Hold 5–10 seconds while keeping knees over toes.
  5. Slide back up, squeezing the thighs and buttocks as you rise.
  6. Repeat.
- **Form cues:** Knees track over the toes and never past them; keep the whole back and hips in contact with the wall; don't sink deep — partial depth protects the kneecap.
- **Common mistakes:** Sliding too deep (increases kneecap pressure); letting knees cave inward; holding the breath during the hold.
- **Typical dosage:** 5–10 second holds × 8–15 repetitions, 1–2 sets, several times per week (Cleveland: 15 reps).
- **Progression:** Longer holds, slightly deeper (still ≤90 degrees), or add a mini-band above the knees. | **Regression:** Shallower bend, shorter holds, feet closer to the wall reduced hold count.
- **Cautions:** Depth is the lever — reduce it immediately if the front of the knee complains; avoid during acutely swollen flares.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-hold` — static partial-squat pose facing the camera; knee flexion angle (hip–knee–ankle) and hip height verify depth and hold duration.
- **Sources:** [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/exercises-for-patellofemoral-pain-syndrome-pfps) (T1); [Mayo Clinic](https://newsnetwork.mayoclinic.org/discussion/recommended-exercises-for-knee-pain-depend-on-the-cause-of-the-pain/) (T1); [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/knee-exercises-for-runners/) (T1); [InformedHealth / IQWiG](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK561509/) (T1)

### Half Squat (Mini Squat)
- **Aliases:** partial squat, knee squat (chair-supported), mini knee bend
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** none (chair or counter for hand support; light dumbbells for progression)
- **Target:** quadriceps, glutes, hamstrings
- **Purpose:** Trains the exact movement pattern of sitting, stairs, and lifting through a knee-friendly partial range; a core strengthening drill in the AAOS program and NHS/Versus Arthritis sheets.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart, toes forward or slightly out; rest fingertips on a chair or counter if needed.
  2. Keep the chest up and look ahead.
  3. Bend hips and knees to lower about 25–30 cm — roughly a quarter-to-half squat (NHS: until your bottom just touches a chair behind you, without sitting).
  4. Pause briefly at the bottom.
  5. Push through the heels to stand tall, tensing thighs and buttocks as you rise.
  6. Repeat.
- **Form cues:** Hips move back as if reaching for a chair; knees track over the toes without passing them; keep the weight through the heels/midfoot.
- **Common mistakes:** Knees collapsing inward; dropping too deep too soon; heels lifting off the floor; leaning far forward.
- **Typical dosage:** 8–15 repetitions, 2–3 sets, 3–5 days per week (NHS: sets of 8 up to 2 × 15; AAOS: 3 × 10).
- **Progression:** Deeper (still comfortable) range, slower tempo, then light dumbbells (AAOS: build toward ~4.5 kg). | **Regression:** Shallower bend, hands on support, or squat down to a high chair seat.
- **Cautions:** Depth stays inside the pain-free range; avoid deep squatting during flares.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — repetitive knee flexion angle (hip–knee–ankle) plus vertical hip displacement are robust front-view signals.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) (T1); [NHS Inform](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/muscle-bone-and-joints/leg-and-foot-problems-and-conditions/exercises-for-knee-problems/) (T1); [Versus Arthritis](https://www.versusarthritis.org/media/21787/kneepain-exercise-sheet.pdf) (T1); [Medical News Today](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/311138) (T3)

### Sit-to-Stand
- **Aliases:** chair stand, chair rise, sit-stand squat
- **Position:** seated to standing
- **Equipment:** chair (cushion to raise the seat as a regression)
- **Target:** quadriceps, glutes; functional leg strength
- **Purpose:** Directly rehearses the most demanded everyday knee task — getting out of a chair — building strength and confidence; used by Versus Arthritis, the Arthritis Foundation, and GLA:D-style functional programs.
- **Steps:**
  1. Sit toward the front of a sturdy chair, feet flat and hip-width apart, heels slightly back under the knees.
  2. Lean the trunk slightly forward ("nose over toes").
  3. Without using your hands, push through your feet and stand fully upright.
  4. Pause, then bend hips and knees to lower yourself slowly back to sitting.
  5. Touch down with control — no plopping.
  6. Repeat.
- **Form cues:** Slow and controlled both directions; knees track over the feet; use the leg muscles, not arm push-off or momentum.
- **Common mistakes:** Rocking/swinging to generate momentum; collapsing back into the seat; knees caving inward on the rise.
- **Typical dosage:** Repeat to comfortable fatigue, rest 1 minute, 3 rounds (Versus Arthritis); or 1–2 sets of 10, building reps over time.
- **Progression:** Lower seat, slower lowering, arms crossed over chest, or single-leg emphasis. | **Regression:** Raise the seat with a cushion; allow light fingertip support.
- **Cautions:** Use a stable chair; keep within comfortable knee bend and stop if pain sharpens.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — large, repetitive change in knee flexion angle (hip–knee–ankle) and hip/shoulder height from a front view; a classic pose-tracked exercise.
- **Sources:** [Versus Arthritis](https://www.versusarthritis.org/about-arthritis/exercising-with-arthritis/exercises-for-healthy-joints/exercises-for-the-knees/) (T1); [Arthritis Foundation](https://www.arthritis.org/health-wellness/healthy-living/physical-activity/getting-started/your-exercise-solution/lower-body-movements/knee-movements) (T1); [GLA:D International](https://gladinternational.org/glad-hip-and-knee/) (T1)

### Step-Up
- **Aliases:** forward step-up, stair step-up
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** step (bottom stair or low block); rail/wall for balance
- **Target:** quadriceps, glutes, calves; single-leg control
- **Purpose:** Builds the single-leg strength and control needed for stairs and uneven ground; appears in NHS, Versus Arthritis, and Cleveland Clinic knee programs as a functional progression.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand facing the bottom step of a staircase (or a low, stable block), hand on the rail if needed.
  2. Place the affected-side foot fully on the step.
  3. Push through that foot to lift your body up, bringing the other foot up to (or tapping) the step.
  4. Step back down slowly with the unaffected foot first, keeping the affected foot on the step (NHS pattern) or alternating leads.
  5. Repeat for the set, then swap leading legs.
- **Form cues:** The whole foot stays on the step; drive up through the leading leg rather than pushing off the floor leg; keep the knee tracking over the toes, not diving inward.
- **Common mistakes:** Bouncing off the trailing leg; knee collapsing inward on the drive; using a step that's too high too soon.
- **Typical dosage:** 1–2 sets of 10 repetitions per leg, 2–4 days per week; build reps before height.
- **Progression:** Higher step, no hand support, add light dumbbells, or slow the step-down phase. | **Regression:** Lower step, fingertip rail support, fewer reps.
- **Cautions:** Keep a support within reach; stop if the kneecap complains on the way down — lowering demands are highest.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — repetitive knee flexion angle (hip–knee–ankle) of the lead leg plus vertical hip displacement; needs framing that keeps head-to-feet visible on the step.
- **Sources:** [NHS Inform](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/muscle-bone-and-joints/leg-and-foot-problems-and-conditions/exercises-for-knee-problems/) (T1); [Versus Arthritis](https://www.versusarthritis.org/media/21787/kneepain-exercise-sheet.pdf) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/knee-arthritis-exercises) (T1)

### Calf Raise
- **Aliases:** heel raise, toe raise (up on toes)
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** none (chair or counter for balance; step edge for progression)
- **Target:** calf muscles (gastrocnemius, soleus)
- **Purpose:** Strong calves help absorb ground impact and control the lower leg, offloading the knee during walking; included in AAOS, Kaiser, and Cleveland Clinic knee programs.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand tall with feet a few inches apart, hands resting lightly on a chair or counter.
  2. Keeping the knees straight (not locked hard), rise up onto the balls of both feet, lifting the heels 2–5 cm.
  3. Hold about 2–6 seconds.
  4. Lower the heels slowly back to the floor.
  5. Repeat.
- **Form cues:** Rise straight up — don't roll onto the outer or inner edge of the feet; use fingertips for balance only; control the descent.
- **Common mistakes:** Bouncing; leaning heavily on the support; letting the ankles wobble outward.
- **Typical dosage:** 8–15 repetitions, 2–3 sets, 3–5 days per week (Kaiser: 6-second holds × 8–12; AAOS: 3 × 10 building to single-leg).
- **Progression:** Single-leg raises, or heels lowering below a step edge for extra range. | **Regression:** Both hands on support, smaller lift, seated calf raises.
- **Cautions:** Keep a support at hand if balance is limited; lower slowly to avoid Achilles irritation.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — repetitive vertical displacement of the ankle/heel landmarks relative to toe position from a front view (ankle plantarflexion signal: knee–ankle–foot); subtle, so needs good framing of the lower body.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Kaiser Permanente](https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.knee-arthritis-exercises.bo1534) (T1); [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/osteoarthritis-knee-exercises-stretches) (T1)

### Resistance-Band Leg Press
- **Aliases:** band leg press, elastic-band press-out, supine leg press
- **Position:** supine or seated (semi-reclined)
- **Equipment:** resistance band
- **Target:** quadriceps, glutes, hamstrings, calves together
- **Purpose:** Reproduces the gym leg-press pattern at home with adjustable elastic load, training all the leg's anti-gravity muscles through a controlled range — recommended by AAOS and the Arthritis Foundation for knee support.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on your back (or sit reclined) and bend one hip and knee, placing the middle of a band around the sole of that foot.
  2. Hold the band ends firmly at your chest/hips with elbows anchored.
  3. Press the foot forward/away until the knee is nearly straight — do not snap it into a lock.
  4. Pause briefly.
  5. Bend the knee slowly back toward you against the band's pull.
  6. Complete the set and switch legs.
- **Form cues:** Keep the band centered on the sole; move slowly in both directions; keep the knee in line with hip and ankle throughout.
- **Common mistakes:** Letting the band recoil the leg back quickly; locking the knee hard at the end; letting the knee drift inward.
- **Typical dosage:** 3 sets of 10 repetitions per leg, 4–5 days per week (AAOS), with a comfortably challenging band.
- **Progression:** Thicker band or shorter band length; slower return phase. | **Regression:** Lighter band, smaller range of knee bend.
- **Cautions:** Check the band for wear and anchor it securely on the foot; keep range pain-free.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — supine/reclined floor position with motion mostly toward/away from the camera; joint angles are unreliable in that orientation.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) (T1); [Arthritis Foundation](https://www.arthritis.org/health-wellness/healthy-living/physical-activity/getting-started/your-exercise-solution/lower-body-movements/knee-movements) (T1)

### Lateral Band Walk
- **Aliases:** banded side steps, monster walk (variant), crab walk
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** resistance band (loop above knees or ankles)
- **Target:** gluteus medius, hip abductors, quads (quarter-squat hold)
- **Purpose:** Trains the outer-hip muscles dynamically so the knee stays aligned during walking and stairs; widely used in patellofemoral pain and knee-stability rehab.
- **Steps:**
  1. Loop a band around both legs — above the knees (easiest), above the ankles, or around the feet (hardest).
  2. Stand with feet hip-width apart and sink into a slight quarter-squat, toes pointing forward.
  3. Step one foot sideways 15–30 cm, keeping the band taut and the trunk level.
  4. Follow with the trailing foot, stopping while the band still has tension.
  5. Take 8–12 controlled steps in one direction.
  6. Return leading with the other leg.
- **Form cues:** Stay low and level — no bobbing; toes keep pointing forward (12 o'clock); do not let the stance knee cave inward as you step.
- **Common mistakes:** Standing up tall between steps; dragging the trailing foot in until the band goes slack; knees collapsing inward.
- **Typical dosage:** 8–12 steps each direction, 2–3 sets, 2–3 days per week.
- **Progression:** Band lower on the legs (ankles/feet), thicker band, deeper stance. | **Regression:** Band above the knees, lighter band, shallower knee bend.
- **Cautions:** Clear floor space; keep steps small and controlled; skip during sharp flare-ups of kneecap pain.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — lateral ankle displacement and stance-width oscillation with a held knee-flexion angle are visible from a front view, though the user may walk out of frame (cue stepping back and forth within frame).
- **Sources:** [NASM](https://blog.nasm.org/fitness/exercises-for-knee-pain) (T2); [Physio Vital / Fysio Fitaal](https://fysiofitaal.nl/en/exercise/lateral-band-walks/) (T3); [Neuroendo PT guide](https://www.neuroendomke.com/exercise-of-the-week-banded-lateral-walking/) (T3)

## Balance & Stability

### Single-Leg Balance
- **Aliases:** single-leg stance, one-leg stand, flamingo stand
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** none (wall or chair nearby for safety)
- **Target:** hip and knee stabilizers, ankle strategy, proprioception
- **Purpose:** Retrains the joint-position sense and stabilizing reflexes that knee pain and osteoarthritis blunt, improving confidence on stairs and uneven ground; balance work is a named component of GLA:D-style neuromuscular programs and NHS knee OA rehab.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand near a wall or sturdy chair on a flat, non-slip surface.
  2. Shift your weight onto the affected leg, keeping that knee very slightly bent.
  3. Lift the other foot just off the floor.
  4. Hold steady, eyes ahead, for 10–30 seconds.
  5. Set the foot down, rest, and repeat; then switch legs.
- **Form cues:** Soft (unlocked) stance knee; hips level — don't let one side drop; look ahead rather than down at your feet.
- **Common mistakes:** Locking the stance knee; gripping the support the whole time instead of hovering near it; letting the pelvis sag sideways.
- **Typical dosage:** Hold 20–30 seconds (start at 5–10 s if needed), 3–5 repetitions per leg, daily; alternatively up to 1-minute holds × 2–3.
- **Progression:** No hand support → eyes looking ahead → eyes closed → stand on a cushion/foam → add small perturbations or head turns. | **Regression:** Fingertip support on wall/chair, shorter holds.
- **Cautions:** Always have support within arm's reach; keep discomfort at or below ~4/10; avoid when dizzy or very fatigued.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-hold` — static one-leg pose from a front view: one ankle elevated relative to the other, stance knee angle, and hip-level (pelvic drop) all checkable; sway of shoulder/hip landmarks can score steadiness.
- **Sources:** [Sussex Community NHS](https://www.sussexcommunity.nhs.uk/patients-and-visitors/resources/patient-resources/knee-osteoarthritis/rehabilitation-exercises) (T1); [University of Michigan Medicine](https://www.med.umich.edu/1libr/PMR/BalanceExercises/StaticStanding_SingleLeg.pdf) (T1); [GLA:D International](https://gladinternational.org/glad-hip-and-knee/) (T1); [Pure Physiotherapy](https://purephysiotherapy.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Knee-Osteoarthritis-Intermediate-Pure.pdf) (T3)

### Side-Lying Hip Adduction
- **Aliases:** inner-thigh lift, bottom-leg raise
- **Position:** side-lying
- **Equipment:** none (optional ankle weight)
- **Target:** hip adductors (inner thigh)
- **Purpose:** Balances inner-thigh strength to support knee alignment; part of the AAOS knee conditioning program.
- **Steps:**
  1. Lie on the side of the leg to be worked, with the top leg bent and its foot placed on the floor in front of the bottom knee (or resting on a chair).
  2. Keep the bottom leg straight.
  3. Lift the bottom leg upward a few inches, leading with the inner thigh.
  4. Hold briefly, lower slowly, repeat, then switch sides.
- **Form cues:** Keep the trunk still and hips stacked; small, controlled lifts; don't let the lower back arch.
- **Common mistakes:** Rolling backward; lifting with momentum; holding the breath.
- **Typical dosage:** 3 sets of 20 per side, 4–5 days per week (AAOS), progressing with ankle weights.
- **Progression:** Ankle weight on the bottom leg. | **Regression:** Fewer reps, no hold.
- **Cautions:** Keep the movement small and pain-free at the groin.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — side-lying with a small bottom-leg lift; heavy landmark occlusion.
- **Sources:** [AAOS OrthoInfo](https://orthoinfo.aaos.org/en/recovery/knee-conditioning-program/) (T1)

### Hamstring Set (Isometric Heel Dig)
- **Aliases:** hamstring isometric, heel-dig set
- **Position:** supine or long-sitting with the knee slightly bent
- **Equipment:** none
- **Target:** hamstrings
- **Purpose:** Activates the hamstrings without joint movement, pairing with quad sets as a gentle warm-up/strength starter for sore knees.
- **Steps:**
  1. Sit or lie with the knee bent about 10–30 degrees, heel on the floor/bed.
  2. Dig the heel down and gently drag it back toward you without letting it actually move.
  3. Feel the back of the thigh tighten; hold 3–10 seconds.
  4. Relax fully and repeat.
- **Form cues:** Press and pull the heel without movement; keep breathing; effort firm but comfortable.
- **Common mistakes:** Letting the heel actually slide; cramping from maximal effort — back off intensity.
- **Typical dosage:** 10 repetitions with 3–10 second holds, 1–2 sessions daily.
- **Progression:** Longer/firmer holds, different knee angles. | **Regression:** Lighter effort.
- **Cautions:** Ease off at the first sign of hamstring cramp.
- **Camera trackability:** `not-trackable` — isometric with no visible motion.
- **Sources:** [Ask Doctor Jo](https://www.askdoctorjo.com/real-time-knee-stretches/) (T3)

### Lateral Step-Up
- **Aliases:** side step-up, lateral step-down (lowering emphasis)
- **Position:** standing
- **Equipment:** step or low block
- **Target:** quadriceps, gluteus medius; single-leg alignment control
- **Purpose:** Adds a sideways challenge to step-ups, emphasizing the hip muscles that keep the knee from diving inward — suggested for runner's knee once forward step-ups feel easy.
- **Steps:**
  1. Stand side-on to a low step with the near foot fully on it.
  2. Push through the stepping leg to lift the body up until that knee is straight.
  3. Touch the far foot lightly to the step or hover it.
  4. Lower slowly back down with control, then repeat and switch sides.
- **Form cues:** Keep the pelvis level; knee tracks over the mid-foot; control the lowering — that's the valuable part.
- **Common mistakes:** Pushing off the floor leg; knee collapsing inward; step too high.
- **Typical dosage:** 8–12 repetitions per side, 1–3 sets, 2–3 days per week.
- **Progression:** Higher step, slower descent, light dumbbells. | **Regression:** Lower step, fingertip support.
- **Cautions:** Have support nearby; stop if the kneecap aches during the descent.
- **Camera trackability:** `trackable-reps` — knee flexion angle (hip–knee–ankle) and vertical hip displacement repeat each rep and are visible from a front view when the user faces the camera side-on to the step.
- **Sources:** [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/exercises-for-patellofemoral-pain-syndrome-pfps) (T1)
